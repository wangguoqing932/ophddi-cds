## Abstract

**Background.** Ophthalmic eye drops reach the systemic circulation, yet the DDInter export used here carries one severity grade per pair and no absorption field; route awareness is partial (Drugs.com, Lexicomp), but no registry-wide absorption model is applied. That gap is unmeasured. **Methods.** We built OphDDI-CDS, a deterministic cascade of 40 rules, an absorption-scaled matrix and a hard constraint, covering 113 ophthalmic and 232 systemic agents. We compared it with database severity across 1,933 matched pairs with graded severity, with three LLM baselines on 118 author-derived cases, and with two clinicians on 12 cases. **Results.** Absorption-aware assessment graded 77.7% of the 1,933 pairs lower than the database (76.3% of assessed pairs), including 352 from high, and 80.3% of those downgrades involved low-tier ophthalmic agents. On the 118-case author-derived reference set, exact agreement was 0.856 under the original frozen labels (101/118; kappa 0.779) and 0.881 after three post-lock label corrections (104/118); on the 40-case frozen blind set it was 0.900 (36/40); the 20 B-only cases scored 18/20 frozen and 20/20 corrected (descriptive, not development-free). 10-token frozen-label agreement was 0.419-0.714. A second configuration changing model, token allowance and sampling gave 0.576-0.737 under corrected labels (rule_context row: pre-correction prompt), with high-risk sensitivity 0.214-0.536; the cascade's sensitivity held at 0.893. On a semi-independent 92-case audit, agreement was 0.674 but a high-risk sensitivity of 0.320 (8 of 25) under applied labels and 0.276 (8 of 29) re-derived, its principal limitation; on the 62 cases whose labels do not depend on its own absorption assumption, agreement was 0.548. A second-configuration baseline scored higher on the full audit set (0.728), confined to the 26 cases whose DDInter statement it retrieves; excluding them, 0.652 vs 0.712 for the cascade. Two clinicians agreed on 11 of 12 cases (kappa 0.840) yet both rated 8 of 12 lower (6 of 12 frozen)—descriptively, not as validation. **Conclusions.** Database severity, evidence-graded rules and clinician ratings assign ophthalmic DDI severity grades differently, and none of the three is a ground truth. An absorption-first deterministic cascade makes those disagreements enumerable, traceable and auditable, but its false-negative profile requires prospective, outcome-linked calibration.

## 1. Introduction

Ophthalmic topical medications are among the most widely prescribed drugs, yet their systemic absorption is frequently underestimated in drug-drug interaction (DDI) screening. Although eye drops are assumed to act locally, systemic absorption after topical administration is well documented [1-3], and several agents reach clinically meaningful exposure; a recent pharmacovigilance analysis of ophthalmic atropine identified systemic safety signals [4]. Timolol is systemically absorbed through the nasolacrimal route and reaches plasma concentrations in the low ng/mL range after topical dosing [5-7]; topical carteolol can cause atrioventricular block [8]; severe reactions to ophthalmic timolol have been linked to CYP2D6 poor metabolizers [9]; and documented events include severe bradycardia with topical timolol plus verapamil [10], and case reports of bradycardia with a case series of bradycardia and syncope [11,12]. Alpha-2 agonists cause central nervous system depression in children [13-16], who are generally more susceptible [17]. Techniques intended to limit nasolacrimal drainage, such as eyelid closure and wiping away excess drops, have been evaluated [18], and the tissue-press technique lowered plasma timolol in a crossover trial [19], whereas in elderly patients on multiple systemic medications additive effects can accumulate into harm [20,21].

The DDInter 2.0 export used here provides one pair-level severity per drug pair and no route or absorption field [22,23]. DDI screening is clinically relevant but methodologically heterogeneous [24], and automated detection remains incomplete [25,26]. Crucially, that export cannot encode the decisive ophthalmic modifier (the magnitude of systemic absorption from topical administration) or the corresponding dose-route correction. Contextualized algorithms exist for patient factors but not for route-specific absorption [27]. Route awareness is not wholly absent: Drugs.com keeps a separate ophthalmic entity for timolol, and Lexicomp asks for the route. What is missing is an explicit registry-wide absorption model, and neither the size of the mismatch nor its relation to clinician judgment has been measured.

Large language models (LLMs) offer flexibility but raise reliability concerns: unconstrained models can hallucinate and are non-reproducible, retrieval-augmented variants inherit the biases of their corpora [28], and prospective evaluations of LLM-based decision support remain limited [29]. Systematic reviews of computerized decision support report modest and variable effects on practice [30,31], and high alert-override rates remain a central adoption problem [32-34].

We therefore built OphDDI-CDS, a deterministic clinical decision-support (CDS) three-layer cascade of 40 mechanism rules (each with a source record), an absorption-scaled class matrix and a hard-constraint layer, covering 113 ophthalmic and 232 systemic agents and grounded in 1,307 approved, source-tagged evidence chunks. The production path makes no LLM call, shows zero variance across temperature conditions, and returns matched rule identifiers with every grade. Our contribution is threefold: we quantify rather than assert the divergence from unadjusted database severity; we provide the instrument that makes the measurement traceable; and we report the evaluation, including two reference sets on which the system agrees poorly, in a form that can be audited and re-run.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Design, data sources and route representation

Evaluation followed a four-phase protocol: rule consistency, development-phase checks, a semi-independent external audit against DDInter, and a two-expert grade review reported as a perspective comparison rather than an independent assessment, since one rater is a member of the corresponding author's research team (Section 2.5). The principal registry-scale analysis was repeated on the 104 topically administered agents (the nine intravitreal, intraocular and surgical agents are reported separately), and three LLM baselines were compared under five temperature conditions.

Route is modelled through two mechanisms rather than a route field: every ophthalmic agent carries one of six systemic-absorption tiers, the proxy for how much of a topical dose reaches the circulation, and the rule layer encodes route-dependent predicates directly, for example a rule requiring the `systemic_absorption_high` flag before it fires.

The registries are summarised in Table 1. Data sources were DDInter 2.0 (302,516 DDI records) [23], FDA ophthalmic product labels, Stockley's Drug Interactions, PubMed, and the British National Formulary [35]. All 1,307 evidence chunks were filtered to approved release and assigned a **source tier**: A regulatory label, B meta-analysis or systematic review, C original study, D in vitro, animal or pharmacokinetic first principles, E class-level mechanism inference. The tiers describe provenance, not strength of evidence - a label statement (A) can be less direct than a systematic review (B) for a specific pair, and an E-tier mechanism can be biologically plausible but unverified - and they are not weights in the engine. The distribution is A = 53, B = 191, C = 215, D = 584, E = 264 (Supplementary Text S1; what each tier can and cannot support in Supplementary Table S16).

### 2.2 The cascade

The production system (`full_system`) is a three-layer deterministic cascade with zero LLM calls (Figure 1; registry counts in Table 1 and evidence-base counts in Section 2.1).

**L1 rule engine.** Forty mechanism rules (additive beta-blockade, beta-blocker-calcium-channel-blocker effects, additive alpha-2 CNS depression, CYP3A4 induction or inhibition, QT prolongation with low-absorption fluoroquinolones; full inventory in Table 2) are evaluated as entity-flag predicates with match-all or match-any semantics, the highest-severity hit retained. The 43 policy entries comprise the 40 rules plus 3 patient-factor policies; rules are evaluated independently, and the only cross-layer composition is the final maximum of the three layers.

**Rule construction, adjudication and provenance.** Candidates were generated from DDInter 2.0 pairs linking an ophthalmic agent to a systemic class, ophthalmic product labels, and published case reports or reviews. Severity was assigned in a fixed order: the DDInter level where the pair is recorded, otherwise the label's warning category, otherwise the consequence expected from the mechanism and the systemic drug's therapeutic index. The inventory was reviewed by the study team and the two clinician authors (consensus adjudication; no Delphi panel, blinded review or independent clinical committee). A three-question audit of all 40 rules - does the source concern the named ophthalmic agent, the pair, and the assigned severity and route? - found 2 with direct ophthalmic pair evidence, 5 with contextual ophthalmic evidence, 23 with class-extrapolated systemic-route evidence and 10 with mechanism-only support; severity is directly supported for 2, inferred for 28 and provisional for 10 (Supplementary Table S9; construction protocol in Supplementary Text S10). Four citations naming an unrelated drug were corrected in this revision (digoxin, warfarin and two low-absorption antiplatelet/anticoagulant rules; Supplementary Text S2). The only post-lock rule-severity change was the dexamethasone CYP3A4 rule, downgraded from medium to low on measured exposure below 1 ng/mL.

**L2 class matrix.** The matrix declares 21 ophthalmic row keys and 34 systemic column keys (33 with defined cells), drawn from the 40 systemic classes, and defines 104 cells: 83 actionable and 21 explicitly marked "none". Eighty-three actionable cells were defined from the rule set, class extrapolation, expert consensus or pharmacological inference; 21 additional cells in the `other` column are explicitly marked none (`not_established`, evidence `none`, manual review), so the matrix contains 104 cells. Each cell records a level, a mechanism, one-line advice and an evidence type. Four upgrade rules require both a class match and a high-absorption or selectivity flag, and an absorption-scaling step forces low, very-low, minimal and none tiers to low on the matrix branch, so no upgrade can raise a low-tier pair. That step is **not empirically validated** against outcome data; it is an author-specified decision grounded in the principle that a dose which does not reach the systemic circulation cannot produce a systemic interaction, and its behavioural consequence is quantified in Section 3.4. Cell levels were reviewed with the rule set by the study team and were not independently adjudicated; all 104 cells are listed in Supplementary Table S14.

**L3 hard constraint and unassessed pairs.** Low or very-low absorption with no rule hit and no matrix signal yields low risk (G19). The hard constraint tests two of these six tiers (low and very-low); minimal and none are mapped to low in the matrix layer. Pairs not caught by G19 that reach neither a rule nor a defined cell return low with `assessment_status = unassessed`; the grade is retained for continuity but is not evidence of safety (Section 3.4). Unknown medications are not silently classified: the engine returns an explicit unknown-drug path and the service raises an error.

**Grade definitions and expected management.** The grades are screening categories, not probabilities of harm: high, severe harm plausible at ophthalmic exposure or a narrow-therapeutic-index victim with a substantial exposure change (action: prescriber or pharmacist review, monitoring, consideration of an alternative); medium, a measurable but usually non-severe effect or a severe mechanism at low exposure (review and monitoring); low, no mechanism expected to be clinically significant at ophthalmic exposure (no action beyond usual care); unassessed, no rule and no matrix cell (manual review; not evidence of safety). Table 6 gives the full operational definitions, clinical significance, expected management and an illustrative pair for each category.

**Absorption tiers.** Each agent carries one of six ordinal exposure bands, defined as the expected systemic exposure after topical administration relative to an oral therapeutic dose: high (at least 10%, or plasma concentrations within the systemic therapeutic range), medium (1-10%), low (below 1% but detectable), very low (below assay quantification), minimal (intravitreal macromolecular agents and intraocular agents with negligible systemic exposure) and none (viscoelastics and tear substitutes). The registry contains 12 high, 20 medium, 59 low, 11 very-low, 7 minimal and 4 none agents. Supplementary Table S5 records the evidence and certainty for all 113 rows: 13 measured (six single agents and seven fixed combinations: five timolol combinations whose tier follows the measured timolol component and two dexamethasone combinations whose tier follows the measured dexamethasone component), 11 label-statement and 89 class-inferred (marked uncertain); for the 13 measured rows the exposure metric is recorded, because fractional bioavailability, plasma concentration and relative exposure are not interchangeable; for the 11 label-statement rows the label claim is recorded without a value. The 13 registry-versus-curation discrepancies reported previously have been resolved rather than deferred: eight curation values were corrected to the registry, dexamethasone and its two fixed combinations were re-tiered from high to medium on peak plasma concentrations below 1 ng/mL (TobraDex label), and dorzolamide and brinzolamide were re-tiered from low to very low because plasma concentrations are below assay quantification (full resolution in Supplementary Table S10). Fixed combinations are decomposed into active components (product tier = highest component tier; rule matching on the union of component mechanisms), the six intravitreal anti-VEGF agents are assigned individually (formats and serum-exposure evidence in Table S5; all minimal), and the nine non-topical agents are reported separately (Section 3.4).

**Patient-specific factors.** The system is pair-level: age, renal function, hepatic function, QT risk, dose, duration, CYP phenotype and instillation technique are not modifiers of the grade. Three contextual policies (elderly, renal impairment, polypharmacy) are returned as separate advisories triggered by narrow-therapeutic-index systemic flags, not applied to the grade; QT risk is represented only through `qt_prolonging` flags and the QT rules, and CYP phenotype only through substrate/inhibitor flags. Supplementary Table S15 maps each factor to its representation, its effect on the output and the remaining gap.

**Intended workflow.** The intended use is a second-reader check at the point of prescribing or medication review (Figure 6). The user enters the ophthalmic agent and the systemic drug; out-of-coverage agents return an explicit error, and for covered pairs the service returns the final grade, decision path, matched rule identifiers, source record and management category: high prompts review before starting, medium prompts review and monitoring, low requires no action beyond usual care, and unassessed pairs are referred for manual review. The workflow is proposed, not evaluated in a clinical setting; no outcome or prescribing data were collected.

The evidence base was embedded with a 2048-dimensional Doubao model and indexed with LightRAG [36] (1,307 documents, 4,956 entities, 6,885 relations); retrieval baselines used BM25 [37] over the same chunks.

### 2.3 Reference and audit data

**L1 author-derived reference set (118 cases).** Three subsets: 20 cases annotated by the study team from named literature sources, 58 guideline-sourced cases covering classes where the routes diverge most, and a procedurally frozen 40-case blind subset. Twenty labels (G01–G20, tagged `T1_literature_consensus`) were annotated from FDA labels, Stockley's, BNF tables and Drugs.com, with supporting statements deposited; five differ from the DDInter severity for the same pair, and the remaining 98 were annotated by the two authors from the same categories of source. The 58 guideline cases were drawn from the evidence that specific rules cite: for 47 the reference label is that rule's severity, and 11 were overridden by hand after review (identifiers in Supplementary Table S1). Agreement on the 47 therefore measures rule fidelity by construction, and the groups are reported separately. The initial labels and blind proposals were drafted without reference to system outputs. After the lock and before the questionnaire, on 31 July 2026, three labels were changed to match the engine's grade: B02 (high→medium), B12 (medium→high) and C17 (low→medium); both versions are reported. The primary analysis uses the labels as frozen, before those corrections; the corrected labels are a sensitivity analysis (Section 3.3), and the 40-case blind subset is reported separately. The subset is procedurally frozen but not development-free: C01–C20 entered the class-matrix loop in v2.1–v2.3, and B01–B20 were also reviewed during development (B02 and B12 were corrected after engine review, B18 and B19 informed matrix design, and other B cases informed rule-gap diagnosis). Neither half is an independent test. B02 and B12 are in the B-only subset, so the 20/20 corrected figure inherits both of those changes and is partly tautological, because both were moved to match the engine's grade; C17 belongs to the C-only subset. DeepSeek-chat was used as an assistive tool for part of the reference-label annotation; all labels were reviewed and verified by the authors, and the model did not have access to system outputs. The seventeen frozen-label disagreements are listed with rationale in Supplementary Table S1; reference-level distributions under the frozen and corrected labels are given in Table 4 and Supplementary Table S8.

**DDInter audit set (92 cases).** A stratified random sample by absorption tier and DDInter severity. The stated rule was to set a case to low when the agent's tier is low or lower, no rule fires and no matrix upgrade applies. As applied, 30 cases were corrected and 62 kept, with three documented deviations (seven corrected cases are in fact reached by a rule; the two aflibercept and four flurbiprofen pairs were kept; full list in Supplementary Text S8). Re-deriving the labels under the stated rule changes 10 of 92, giving 61 of 92 (0.663) with sensitivity 8 of 29, against 62 of 92 (0.674) and 8 of 25 under the applied labels; both sets are reported in full in Table 5. The audit is therefore semi-independent: the severity component is third-party, but the absorption correction shares the system's assumption. A development set (200 DDInter-derived cases), an external co-mention set (70 cases) and an internal development set (78 cases) served as development gates, not validation; the first two carry unadjusted database labels (Section 3.2), and the internal set overlaps 78 of the 118 L1 cases with identical labels. The registry-scale comparison uses the 1,933 pairs with a graded DDInter severity; the export contains 4,546 registry-matched pairs, of which 2,613 carry Level `Unknown` and are excluded.

### 2.4 Comparators and metrics

`pure_llm` makes one LLM call with a domain-general prompt and no retrieved evidence. `naive_rag` adds BM25 retrieval over the same evidence chunks (`BM25Okapi`, k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75; top-k = 3 passages, each truncated to 400 characters; the query includes the drug names - full prompt in Supplementary Text S3). `rule_context` (method id `lightrag` in the deposited code, retained for continuity) adds a knowledge-graph context block rendered by `PreciseKG.full_kg_context()`, which reads the registries and rule configuration rather than the vector index: the drug profiles, the matched rule identifiers with their severities, and, for low-absorption pairs with no matched rule, an explicit instruction that the grade must be low. The baseline therefore receives part of the rule layer, so the gap between it and `full_system` understates the cascade's advantage over a retrieval baseline that does not receive rule hints. Two prompt details limit the baseline comparison: the shared system prompt lists dexamethasone as >50% after the tier was corrected to medium (1-10%), and configuration 2 `rule_context` uses the pre-correction context block. Configuration 1 `rule_context` was re-run with corrected exposure bands (high >=10%, medium 1-10%, low <1%) and the absorption gate; `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` were not re-run. The LLM baseline comparison therefore mixes a corrected `rule_context` row with uncorrected rows and is not a clean test of retrieval augmentation; the deterministic cascade and its reference and audit results are unaffected.

LLM baselines used `deepseek-chat` with a 10-token cap and no chain-of-thought prompting (temperatures 0.0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9 and 1.2; run configuration and file-level fingerprints in Supplementary Text S5). Five conditions × four methods × the 480 cases in four datasets produced 9,600 rows with zero API failures. Configuration 2 used `glm-5.3-flash` with `max_tokens` 3000, temperature 0.0 and a majority vote over five repeated samples. It differs from configuration 1 in model, token allowance and sampling simultaneously; comparisons between them therefore describe two configurations, not the effect of output budget or of reasoning.

Metrics were exact three-level agreement, high-risk sensitivity and specificity, high-risk F1, and Cohen's kappa [38] with Landis–Koch interpretation [39]. The positive class is high versus non-high; the reported specificity is therefore non-high specificity, and medium-to-low errors are not captured by it. Method differences were tested with the exact two-sided McNemar test on discordant pairs at condition 0; intervals are Wilson, Clopper–Pearson or bootstrap as appropriate. No power calculation was performed. Full parameter lists and prompts are in Supplementary Text S3 and S5.

### 2.5 Expert grade review

Two ophthalmologists reviewed 12 cases under a grade-review protocol (Supplementary Table S3). Both are clinicians of the Department of Ophthalmology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, and one is a member of the corresponding author's research team, so we use "perspective comparison" rather than "independent assessment". The instrument displayed the drug pair, a short scenario, the proposed reference grade and a system grade with a one-line rationale, and the expert recorded their own judgment on the same three-level scale; because both grades were visible, this is a grade review rather than a blinded assessment, and the cases were constructed around the system's outputs, so the exercise cannot serve as independent validation. Ratings were collected on 3 August 2026 and transcribed on 8 August; expert 1's questionnaire is deposited as a scan and both sets as transcribed records. Two clinicians are a purposive panel, not a sample of clinical opinion, and the exercise is reported as descriptive. The independent reviewer of the 30 audit disagreements (Section 3.5) is a third ophthalmologist, not one of these two raters, and did not take part in the 12-case exercise.

### 2.6 Intended use and software

The system is a research-grade, mechanism-based second reader. It is not intended to replace clinical or pharmacy judgment, out-of-coverage medications are rejected with an explicit error, and it is not marketed as a medical device. The model does not encode dose, duration, patient susceptibility beyond three patient-factor policies, or local ocular interactions; those remain clinical-judgment inputs. Pairs outside rule and matrix coverage that are not caught by G19 are returned as low with `assessment_status = unassessed`, not as assessed low-risk pairs.

The implementation is Python 3.11 (`ophthalmic_ddi_cds_agent`). Source code, knowledge base and evaluation scripts are available at https://github.com/wangguoqing932/ophddi-cds (release tag `v3.0-frontiers`, archived at DOI 10.5281/zenodo.23439606), with a synthetic demonstration dataset and a one-command entry point. Versions are pinned by SHA-256 fingerprints in the repository-root `MANIFEST.sha256`. The report follows TRIPOD+AI [40] and DECIDE-AI [41] reporting guidance where applicable (Supplementary Text S6).

## 3. Results


### 3.1 System construction

The knowledge base comprises 113 ophthalmic agents (104 topically administered; 9 intravitreal, intraocular or surgical) and 232 systemic medications (Supplementary Table S4). It holds 1,307 approved evidence chunks (A = 53, B = 191, C = 215, D = 584, E = 264; Supplementary Text S1) and a LightRAG index over the same documents. The rule engine comprises 40 mechanism rules, each with a source record graded in Supplementary Table S9, plus 3 patient-factor policies listed in Table 2c; the engine has zero policy errors (Table 2; Supplementary Table S6). Method performance on the primary reference set, the 40-case frozen blind subset, the DDInter audit and configuration 2 is tabulated in Table 3; the development-phase sets are reported in Section 3.2. The class matrix defines 104 cells across 21 ophthalmic row keys and 33 columns with defined cells; reference-level distributions are given in Table 4. Figure 2 summarises the method comparison, Figure 3 the clinician review and Figure 4 representative outputs.

### 3.2 Development-phase reference results

On the internal development set (78 cases) sensitivity was 0.882 (15 of 17) and specificity 0.984 under the gated primary analysis; under the ungated sensitivity analysis it was 1.000 and 0.984. The set is a development gate because 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap it with identical labels; the only two high-label cases lost to the gate are the bromfenac pairs G137 and G138. Against the unadjusted database labels of the development set (200 cases) the system achieved 0.240 exact agreement (sensitivity 0.069, specificity 0.977), and against the external co-mention labels (70 cases, audited as noisy) 0.443 (0.077, 0.935). These low values quantify the absorption correction relative to unadjusted database labels and are not interpretable as performance.

### 3.3 Agreement with the author-derived reference set (118 cases)

On this set the reference labels and the rules draw on the same literature, so agreement measures the internal consistency of an evidence-graded encoding rather than agreement against an independent standard (Table 3, Figure 2; Supplementary Table S7); we use "exact agreement" rather than "accuracy" throughout.

Under the original frozen labels, `full_system` agreed exactly on 101 of 118 cases (0.856; Wilson 95% CI 0.781-0.908), with high-risk sensitivity 0.857 (24 of 28; CI 0.685-0.943), specificity 0.978 and kappa 0.779 (bootstrap CI 0.678-0.870), with zero variance across conditions. After the three post-lock label corrections the figures are 104 of 118 (0.881), sensitivity 0.893, specificity 0.989 and kappa 0.818; the corrected version is a sensitivity analysis, not the primary result. On the 40-case frozen blind set, agreement was 36 of 40 (0.900; CI 0.769-0.960), sensitivity 0.818 (9 of 11) and specificity 0.966; under the corrected labels, 39 of 40. Within this set, the 20 B-only cases scored 18 of 20 under the frozen labels and 20 of 20 under the corrected labels; this is descriptive, and neither half is development-free. The 20 C-only cases, which entered the class-matrix loop, scored 18 of 20 and 19 of 20. The B-only half is reported separately for transparency, but it is not an independent test; it is small and its labels are study-team annotations.

The baselines were weaker under the same frozen labels: `rule_context` 0.714 (SD 0.006), `pure_llm` 0.478 (0.026) and `naive_rag` 0.419 (0.017), with high-risk sensitivities 0.593, 0.779 and 0.764. Exact McNemar tests at condition 0 were significant against each baseline (discordant pairs 48 to 2 versus `pure_llm`, 54 to 4 versus `naive_rag`, 19 to 3 versus `rule_context`; all p < 0.001), and the gradient held across all five temperature conditions. All seventeen disagreements are listed with rationale in Supplementary Table S1; two (G121, G122) follow from the dexamethasone rule downgrade and two (G137, G138) from the absorption gate on `nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism`, both changes introduced in this revision. The 0.142 gap to the best baseline is the margin over a comparator that already receives the matched rules (Section 2.4), so it should not be read as the contribution of retrieval augmentation alone.

Per subset, the frozen blind cases reached 36 of 40, the T1 literature subset 20 of 20 and the guideline subset 45 of 58 (rule fidelity 40 of the 47 cases whose label equals the cited rule's severity; hand-adjudicated 5 of 11). The 0.856 headline is therefore a weighted average of rule fidelity, hand-adjudicated agreement and agreement on the 60 T1 and frozen cases (56/60), not a single independent agreement estimate. No independently adjudicated or development-free test set exists; the 20 B-only cases are reported separately but are too small to support a clinical performance claim.

### 3.4 Registry-scale divergence

This result does not depend on the reference labels, since both sides are pre-specified: DDInter supplies a single pair-level severity without route or absorption information, and the cascade an absorption-aware grade. Of the 1,933 registry-matched pairs with a graded DDInter severity (2,613 `Unknown` pairs were excluded), 1,502 (77.7%) were graded lower by the system, including 352 downgraded from high (34 to medium, 318 to low) and 1,150 from medium to low. A further 65 (3.4%) were upgraded, so the two perspectives differ for 81.1% of pairs. Of all downgrades, 80.3% (1,206 of 1,502) involved low-tier ophthalmic agents; this is a composition statistic, not a causal decomposition. This is a difference in classification, not evidence that either perspective is wrong.

The decision paths are unevenly distributed: 504 pairs (26.1%) were graded by a rule, 507 (26.2%) by a defined matrix cell, 746 (38.6%) by the G19 hard constraint and 176 (9.1%) by the default path, which returns `assessment_status = unassessed`. Excluding those 176, the assessed subset contains 1,757 pairs, of which 1,341 (76.3%) were downgraded. The 176 unassessed pairs are listed in Supplementary Table S11; they include 151 DDInter-medium, 10 DDInter-high and 15 DDInter-low pairs, so the default path is a coverage gap: its low grade is not evidence of safety and does not resolve these high-risk pairs. In the output and in Figure 6 these pairs are labelled `unassessed` and referred for manual review; they are never presented as assessed low-risk pairs.

Restricting to the 104 topically administered agents leaves 1,930 pairs, of which 1,499 (77.7%) were downgraded and 350 fell from high; the three pairs involving the nine intravitreal, intraocular or surgical agents are reported separately in Supplementary Table S12. The divergence is therefore a property of the topical-ophthalmic pairs in the registry, not of the three non-topical pairs. Two caveats bound this measurement: it quantifies divergence, not error (that the two perspectives rarely coincide does not make the database ratings wrong), and the tiers driving it are an ordinal exposure model (Section 2.2), so the measurement is a property of an explicitly stated model rather than an independent pharmacokinetic measurement.

### 3.5 Semi-independent DDInter audit (92 cases)

On the audit set `full_system` reached 0.674 exact agreement (62/92; Wilson CI 0.573-0.761) and kappa 0.428 against the applied absorption-corrected reference labels (specificity 0.970). Under the re-derived labels the figures are 0.663 (61/92; CI 0.562-0.751), kappa 0.434, specificity 0.968 and high-risk sensitivity 0.276 (8 of 29). Both sets are reported in full in Table 5 and Supplementary Table S2; the applied labels are the primary analysis and the re-derived labels the pre-specified sensitivity analysis. Against uncorrected DDInter labels agreement was 0.380 (35/92). Restricting to the 62 cases kept at third-party DDInter severity gives 34 of 62 (0.548); of the 30 corrected cases the system agreed on 28 (93%). Baselines on the applied labels were `pure_llm` 0.630, `rule_context` 0.602 and `naive_rag` 0.535; McNemar tests were not significant versus `rule_context` (10 versus 3; p = 0.092), `naive_rag` or `pure_llm`. The cascade did not differ significantly from the `rule_context` baseline (p = 0.092); its exact agreement and sensitivity were higher, while specificity was slightly lower.

High-risk sensitivity was 0.320 under the applied labels (8 of 25; CI 0.172-0.516) and 0.276 under the re-derived labels (8 of 29). That this figure is low in absolute terms is the principal limitation. The 30 disagreements with the applied labels were classified by the authors into 21 downgrades of medium-, low- or minimal-tier agents, 5 downgrades of high-tier agents that the absorption model does not justify (timolol with theophylline, timolol with epinephrine and timolol with spironolactone through the matrix path; scopolamine with topiramate and carteolol with diazepam through the unassessed default path), and 4 over-grades. The five high-absorption-tier downgrades remain the clearest author-identified subset (three DDInter-high and two DDInter-medium), but they are not the whole clinically critical set: 12 disagreements are on the unassessed default path (10 route-consistent, 2 unsupported), adding four DDInter-high pairs (D3-026, D3-029, D3-033, D3-080) whose low grade is not evidence of safety.

To test that classification, an independent ophthalmologist (a third ophthalmologist, not one of the two raters in Section 2.5; not an author; blinded to the author class, decision path and matched rules; 14 November 2026) reclassified all 30 from the same pair, reference label, system grade, tier and direction. Exact agreement with the author classification was 14 of 30 (46.7%); on the 28 cases that fell within the author's three classes it was 14 of 28 (50.0%, kappa 0.271). The reviewer classified 14 of the 21 author route-consistent downgrades as unsupported undergrading, kept all five author unsupported undergradings as unsupported, and considered two of the four author over-grades to be supported upgrades. The independent view is therefore more conservative and does not reproduce the 21/5 split; it reinforces, rather than resolves, the false-negative concern. The author classification is retained as a descriptive categorization, and both columns are reported in Supplementary Table S2. These are disagreements with reference labels; without outcome data they are not counts of missed adverse events.

Flurbiprofen illustrates how the absorption model interacts with third-party labels. It is formulated at 0.03%, the lowest concentration of any ophthalmic NSAID in the registry, so correcting its tier moved its methotrexate pair and three related antithrombotic pairs further from the DDInter label rather than closer. For a screening tool this false-negative profile is the clinically critical number.

### 3.6 Second configuration (different model, token allowance and sampling)

We also ran the three baselines in configuration 2 (Section 2.4), which differs from the primary comparison in model, token allowance and sampling simultaneously; it therefore describes a second setting, not an isolated effect of output budget or reasoning.

Label agreement was higher in configuration 2: on the 118-case set, under the corrected labels used for this comparison, `pure_llm` rose from 0.475 to 0.576 and `naive_rag` from 0.415 to 0.585, while the `rule_context` row was 0.737 in configuration 2 but 0.719 in the corrected-prompt configuration-1 run, so the two are not directly comparable. High-risk sensitivity fell sharply and specificity rose to approximately 1.000 (Table 3): sensitivity moved from 0.771/0.757/0.621 to 0.250/0.214/0.536, while the cascade held at 0.893 on the corrected labels used for this comparison. The mechanism is a shift in the predicted label distribution, not better discrimination: on the audit set the three baselines assigned high risk to 2.2%, 10.9% and 6.5% of cases, where the reference labels contain 27.2%, and because the set is dominated by low and medium grades this conservatism inflates exact agreement. On the 118-case L1 set, by majority vote the baselines identified 7, 6 and 15 of the 28 reference-high cases, against 25 for the cascade. We therefore read the agreement increase as a property of configuration 2, not as evidence that reasoning improves ophthalmic DDI screening.

Two further results bear on the primary comparison. The ordering between retrieval methods reverses: under the 10-token configuration `naive_rag` scored below `pure_llm`, whereas in configuration 2 it scores at or above it on both sets (0.585 versus 0.576; 0.728 versus 0.609), so the 10-token ordering is a property of the constrained configuration. And on the audit set configuration-2 `naive_rag` reaches 0.728 exact agreement and 0.400 sensitivity, both above the cascade - but that advantage is confined to cases whose own DDInter statement the retriever can reach: with those 26 excluded it falls to 43 of 66 (0.652), below the cascade's 47 of 66 (0.712), and its sensitivity falls from 9 of 10 to 1 of 15. Its sensitivity is also unstable across repeats (0.280 to 0.480), whereas the cascade returns the same grade every time.

**Retrieval overlap.** The 1,307-chunk corpus contains 500 DDInter severity statements, and the audit reference labels for the 62 third-party cases are the DDInter values. We therefore measured, before comparing methods, how often each method's top-3 passages contain the pair's own statement: 26 of 92 audit cases (19 of the 62 kept, 7 of the 30 corrected). `pure_llm` and `rule_context` have no DDInter-statement retrieval channel and zero overlap by construction; results with and without the 26 cases are reported for the cascade in Table 5 and for all methods in Supplementary Table S13.

### 3.7 Determinism and temperature sensitivity

`full_system` is deterministic: all five temperature conditions returned identical predictions (SD = 0.000), a determinism check rather than a robustness claim. Figure 5 shows the per-layer latency trace, in which the cascade completes in under 2 ms with no model call. The LLM baselines varied across conditions on the L1 set under the frozen labels. `rule_context` exact agreement ranged from 0.703 to 0.720 (SD 0.006), `pure_llm` from 0.449 to 0.517 (SD 0.026) and `naive_rag` from 0.390 to 0.441 (SD 0.017), with a similar pattern on the audit set (SD 0.005–0.039).

### 3.8 Exploratory clinician grade review (12 cases)

Two ophthalmologists rated 12 cases with the proposed reference grade and a displayed system grade both visible (Figure 3; Section 2.5). Inter-rater agreement was 11 of 12 (kappa 0.840, 95% bootstrap CI 0.526–1.000; quadratic-weighted 0.875). Agreement with the displayed grades was 2 of 12 for expert 1 and 1 of 12 for expert 2; against the engine grade, 4 of 12 and 3 of 12 (per-expert kappa values and quadratic-weighted figures are in Figure 3; per-case ratings are in Supplementary Table S3). The comparisons differ because for R10 and R11 the displayed grade was not the engine's grade. Against the displayed (post-lock corrected) reference grade each expert rated 8 of 12 cases lower (discordant judgments 8 of 8 and 8 of 9); against the frozen labels used in the primary analysis, each rated 6 of 12 lower, because two cases (R02, R03) were among the three post-lock corrections. Neither rated any of the four displayed-high cases as high, with ratings generally lower than the engine grade rather than randomly distributed.

Across all twelve cases the proposed reference grade equals the deposited engine grade, so the expert-versus-reference and expert-versus-displayed comparisons cannot separate the reference grade from the engine; the exercise is a comparison against the assigned grade, not a test of either. For R10 and R11 the questionnaire displayed medium, a class-level hint written by the study team rather than the engine's output; the engine grades both pairs low, matching the reference grade. All twelve cases map to L1 cases; eleven are from the frozen blind subset and one (R12) is the literature-annotated G08 (per-case mapping and reference source in Supplementary Table S3; lock hashes and correction commits in the deposited case files), one case (C17/R03) involves dexamethasone, whose tier and rule changed in this revision, but its engine grade remained medium; for all twelve pairs the grades shown to the experts are unchanged. Reporting both ways gives 39 of 40 and 104 of 118 under the corrected labels, and 36 of 40 and 101 of 118 under the frozen labels used in the primary analysis.

The exercise is not a validation: both grades were visible, one expert is a study-team member, the cases were constructed around the system's outputs, and the sample is purposive. Two clinicians cannot represent clinical opinion generally, and the design cannot separate a perspective gap from case-selection effects. What it supports is that the two raters did not reproduce the assigned grades, not an error rate.

### 3.9 Comparison with route-aware resources

To separate the contribution of the rules and matrix from a blanket route correction, we constructed a route-aware DDInter filter (RA-DDInter): if the ophthalmic agent's tier is low or lower, the grade is low; otherwise the DDInter severity is retained. RA-DDInter uses the same tier table as OphDDI-CDS and therefore is not an independent exposure model; it is the simplest route-aware resource that can be built from DDInter.

On the 42 L1 cases for which DDInter has a severity record, raw DDInter agrees exactly on 17 of 42 (0.405), RA-DDInter on 21 of 42 (0.500) and the full cascade on 32 of 42 (0.762); high-risk sensitivity is 5, 3 and 9 of 13, and specificity 17, 28 and 29 of 29 (Table 7). The route filter raises specificity by suppressing all low-tier pairs, but it also suppresses reference-high pairs; the rules and matrix recover most of that sensitivity. Across the 1,933 registry-matched pairs with a graded DDInter severity, RA-DDInter and the cascade agree on 1,489 (77.0%); the cascade is higher on 148 pairs (rules or matrix upgrades) and lower on 296 (194 medium-tier and 102 high-tier pairs whose rule, matrix or default path fell below the DDInter severity; no low-tier pairs, because RA-DDInter already assigns low there).

To test the route-specific premise against an external resource, we queried Lexicomp through the 2018 UpToDate interface (application version 1.1; OpenMed-provided; accessed 11 November 2026) for the same 42 pairs. Each pair was queried by drug name only; the ophthalmic product entry was used when the interface exposed one, otherwise the generic ingredient entry was used and flagged (35 ophthalmic-specific cases; generic entries for apraclonidine, bromfenac and voriconazole). No cascade grade or reference label was sent to the interface, and ratings were retrieved before the comparison was computed. The ophthalmic entry was lower in all 21 of the 35 pairs with both entries: 18 pairs had no interaction at the ophthalmic entry but C, D or X at the systemic entry, and three were C versus D. Lexicomp returned C for 15 pairs, B for one, X for one and no interaction for 25. Because C is a broad monitoring category that spans our high and medium grades, the three-level comparison is mapping-dependent: exact agreement was 17 of 42 with C mapped to medium and 18 of 42 with C mapped to high, and high-risk sensitivity was 0 of 13 and 8 of 13, respectively. Under a binary actionable definition (C/D/X), Lexicomp flagged 16 of 42 pairs, capturing 16 of 32 reference non-low pairs (sensitivity 0.500) and none of the 10 reference-low pairs (specificity 1.000; exact 26 of 42, κ 0.323; Supplementary Table S18b). The direction is consistent with the exposure model, but product-level entries may not capture systemic absorption from topical NSAIDs, so the absence of an interaction is not evidence of safety.

The comparison has three limits: RA-DDInter uses the same DDInter data and the same tier table, so it isolates the incremental value of the rules and matrix, not the validity of the tier model; the 42 reference labels are a subset of the frozen author-derived L1 reference set, not a separate adjudication, so agreement with Lexicomp measures alignment with our exposure model rather than clinical truth; and the accessible Lexicomp instance is the 2018 UpToDate interface, not a current licensed release. The 42-case subset is therefore a structured external check, not a benchmark against a current commercial database (Supplementary Tables S18 and S18b).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

Three perspectives on ophthalmic-systemic risk (unadjusted database severity, evidence-graded rules and the two clinician raters) assign severity grades differently, and this study measures that difference on overlapping pair sets. Absorption-aware assessment graded 77.7% of the registry-matched pairs lower than the database and a further 3.4% higher, so the two differ for 81.1% of pairs. Evidence-graded rules and clinician ratings also diverged: the two ophthalmologists agreed closely with each other (kappa 0.840) yet each rated 8 of 12 cases below the engine grade (Section 3.8); because the panel was not independent and the cases were built around the system's outputs, this is a perspective comparison rather than a validation.

The quantitative results bound that claim rather than carry it. Under the 10-token constrained configuration, the cascade's exact agreement was higher than the three baselines (McNemar p < 0.001), but this is not a clean test of retrieval augmentation; on the audit set it led on exact agreement, with specificity comparable to the `rule_context` baseline (0.970 vs 0.979; Section 3.5). Its high-risk sensitivity was, however, low in absolute terms (0.320 applied, 0.276 re-derived), below `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` though above `rule_context`, and it did not differ significantly from the first two; in configuration 2 one baseline exceeded it. Because 47 of the 58 guideline labels are the cited rule's own severity, the 0.856 headline is best read as internal consistency rather than independent agreement; the audit sensitivity bounds what the system can claim as a safety screen today.

### 4.2 What we add relative to prior work

Against general-purpose DDI databases, our system makes the ophthalmic absorption factor an explicit modelling component: a tier on every agent, an absorption-scaling step inside the matrix layer, and a hard constraint; the final grade is the maximum of the three layers. In the primary analysis, `nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism` is gated on absorption and cannot fire when the ophthalmic tier is low or lower. The gate was introduced in this revision because the ungated rule produced 95 high grades in low-tier pairs across the full grid, 68 within the 1,933 graded-severity pairs, and the audit over-grade of bromfenac with enalapril (reduced from high to medium, not eliminated). With the gate, 157 low-tier pairs still receive medium grades through other rules across the full grid (83 within the 1,933 graded-severity pairs), but none receives high. Removing the gate raises frozen-label agreement from 0.856 (101/118) to 0.873 (103/118) and restores the 95 high grades; the ungated results are reported as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S19). The agreement gain is expected because 47 of the 58 guideline labels encode the rule's own severity, so the ungated rule is partly fitting its own source. We keep the gate as the primary behaviour because it is consistent with the absorption model, and report the cost rather than select the rule that maximises agreement with the reference set. The external Lexicomp check points in the same direction: in all 21 pairs with both entries, the ophthalmic entry was graded lower than the systemic entry (Section 3.9), although the accessible interface is a 2018 edition and the comparison is not a current-database benchmark.

Against LLM and retrieval-augmented approaches, the practical difference is not label agreement alone, which favours the cascade on L1 and the full audit set but not on the label-independent subset. It is the sensitivity–specificity profile and the reproducibility of the decision. Machine-learning predictors built on molecular structure [42] or knowledge graphs [43] are trained on DDI records that do not model route-specific absorption. The cascade is deterministic by construction, so reproducibility is a design property, and every decision returns matched rule IDs, a decision path and the rule's source record where available.

Because the DDInter export carries one pair-level severity and no route or absorption field, agreement with it quantifies the effect of the absorption correction rather than accuracy; the unadjusted-label agreements of Section 3.2 are therefore reported as a quantification of the adjustment rather than as performance.

### 4.3 Exposure is not risk

The absorption tier models systemic exposure; it does not model the probability of a clinical interaction, and the relationship between the two depends on the mechanism and the therapeutic index (Supplementary Table S17). For reversible pharmacodynamic effects such as beta-blockade, lower exposure usually means a smaller effect, but a potent agent or a narrow-therapeutic-index victim can still be clinically important. For pharmacokinetic interactions, risk depends on the fractional change in the victim drug's exposure, not on the ophthalmic agent's absolute exposure; a small degree of CYP or P-gp inhibition can matter for warfarin, digoxin, tacrolimus or cyclosporine. For threshold or idiosyncratic reactions, exposure-response is not linear and low exposure does not guarantee absence of risk, and cumulative toxicity depends on duration, which the pair-level model does not represent. Even minimal-exposure intravitreal agents are not automatically risk-free: a pharmacovigilance analysis reported adverse events with standard- and high-dose aflibercept [44], although such signals cannot establish causality.

This distinction bounds the 77.7% divergence: it is a difference in classification produced by the exposure model, not evidence that the downgraded pairs are safe or that alerts were inappropriate. The 77.7% is computed over the 1,933 pairs with a graded severity and includes 161 downgrades on the unassessed default path; among the 1,757 assessed pairs the share is 76.3%. It also explains the audit's false-negative profile: the five author-identified unsupported downgrades are high-tier agents whose risk is not absorption-limited, and the independent review classified 14 additional downgrades as unsupported, so low-tier downgrades cannot be assumed plausible merely because exposure is low. Whether a downgrade is defensible depends on mechanism; the grade is a screening flag, the management category in Table 6 is the clinical action, and it remains conditional on dose, duration and patient factors.

### 4.4 Limitations

**Absorption-tier assignment.** The tiers are the most load-bearing variable. This revision resolves the 13 registry-versus-curation discrepancies (revision provenance audit in Supplementary Text S9) and documents every tier in a structured evidence table (Supplementary Table S5): 13 measured, 11 label-statement and 89 class-inferred (marked uncertain). Dexamethasone and its two fixed combinations were re-tiered from high to medium on measured plasma concentrations below 1 ng/mL; the correction changed individual case agreement in both directions, and we report the net effect rather than retain a tier chosen to fit the labels. The remaining uncertainty is substantial: 89 of 113 tiers are class inferences, and the ordinal bands are not a substitute for measured exposure.

**Unassessed pairs and unmodelled inputs.** The default path returns low with `assessment_status = unassessed`; 176 of the 1,933 graded-severity pairs (9.1%) follow it, including 151 DDInter-medium, 10 DDInter-high and 15 DDInter-low pairs. These are coverage gaps, not evidence of safety. The model does not encode dose, duration, patient susceptibility beyond three patient-factor policies, systemic-to-ocular drug transfer [45], or local ocular interactions; those remain clinical inputs.

**Registry-scale denominator.** The DDInter export records `Unknown` for 2,613 of 4,546 registry-matched pairs. These were excluded, so the 77.7% figure describes pairs with a graded severity (Major, Moderate or Minor), not all matched pairs; its generalization to `Unknown` pairs is unknown.

**Rule and matrix construction.** The rule set and the class-matrix levels were built and reviewed by the study team, with consensus adjudication and no formal Delphi panel, blinded review or independent clinical committee. The support level of each rule is recorded (Supplementary Table S9) and all matrix cells are listed (Supplementary Table S14), but the severity assignments remain expert judgements, and 157 low-tier pairs still receive medium grades through non-gated rules (Section 4.2).

**Patient-level factors.** The system is pair-level: age, renal and hepatic function, QT risk, dose, duration, CYP phenotype and instillation technique are not modifiers of the grade (Supplementary Table S15), and the workflow in Figure 6 is proposed, not evaluated in a clinical setting.

**Bounded clinical impact.** The scarcity of direct evidence admits two explanations: clinically detectable systemic effects may be uncommon, or publication and ascertainment bias may operate; the present data cannot distinguish them. If the true risk gradient clusters at the low end the system may over-grade, while audit sensitivity of 0.320 (0.276 re-derived) shows it can also under-grade. No outcome data are available, so predictive value for adverse events cannot be estimated. The 77.7% divergence is a property of the stated absorption model, not evidence that alerts were inappropriate or that prescribing would improve.

**Validation.** The L1 reference set is heterogeneous (composition in Section 2.3): a literature-derived proxy rather than an independently adjudicated standard. The 40-case frozen set was labelled without reference to system outputs, but both halves were reviewed during development: C01–C20 entered the class-matrix loop in v2.1–v2.3, and B01–B20 informed rule-gap diagnosis and matrix design. No development-free subset remains. Three labels were corrected toward the engine's grades after the lock (reported both ways, Section 3.3), and 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap the development set with identical labels. No independently adjudicated test set exists, and the two-expert review cannot serve as one. The 0.856 headline is therefore an agreement estimate against an author-derived reference set, not a clinical performance estimate.

### 4.5 Future directions

Six directions follow. **Independent adjudication:** a prospectively locked test set adjudicated by blinded reviewers, including clinical pharmacy expertise; the present 20 B-only subset is too small. **Clinical calibration:** a larger expert panel or outcome-linked retrospective data to determine whether a one-tier offset applies to specific tiers or classes, with the audit false-negative pairs as first targets. **False-negative surveillance:** pharmacovigilance mining and prospective monitoring of high-absorption agents to test mechanism-derived rules against outcomes. **Coverage and scope:** extend the registry, integrate the existing unassessed status into prospective workflow evaluation, and transfer the class-matrix architecture to dermatologic, inhaled and intranasal domains. **Route-aware comparison:** a prospective workflow evaluation and a comparison with a current licensed Lexicomp release; the present comparison uses a 2018 edition and the RA-DDInter filter is a proof of concept. **Patient-level model:** integrating eGFR, genotype, QTc, dose, duration and instillation technique would move the system from pair-level screening to patient-level risk estimation.

## 5. Conclusions

We developed OphDDI-CDS, an absorption-first deterministic cascade for ophthalmic-systemic DDI screening, and used it to compare three ways of assigning severity grades. Against DDInter severity, absorption-aware assessment graded 77.7% of the 1,933 pairs with a graded DDInter severity lower, with 80.3% of those downgrades involving low-tier ophthalmic agents. The cascade reached 0.856 exact agreement on the 118-case author-derived reference set under the frozen labels, 0.881 after three post-lock corrections, and 0.900 on the 40-case frozen blind set (18/20 in the 20 B-only cases; descriptive, not an independent test). On a semi-independent audit set it reached 0.674 exact agreement but a high-risk sensitivity of only 0.320 (8 of 25; 0.276 re-derived); that sensitivity, together with a reference set that is part study-team and part author annotation and an independent review that agreed with only 14 of 30 disagreement classifications, bounds what the system can claim today. Ophthalmic DDI risk lacks a shared grading standard: disagreement was present in every comparison of database severity, evidence-graded rules and clinician ratings, and evaluation should measure that disagreement rather than assume any one is correct. Prospective, outcome-linked calibration is the next step.
