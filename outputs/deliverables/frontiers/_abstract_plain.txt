Background. Ophthalmic eye drops reach the systemic circulation, yet the DDInter export used here carries one severity grade per pair and no absorption field; route awareness is partial (Drugs.com, Lexicomp), but no registry-wide absorption model is applied. That gap is unmeasured. Methods. We built OphDDI-CDS, a deterministic cascade of 40 rules, an absorption-scaled matrix and a hard constraint, covering 113 ophthalmic and 232 systemic agents. We compared it with database severity across 1,933 matched pairs with graded severity, with three LLM baselines on 118 author-derived cases, and with two clinicians on 12 cases. Results. Absorption-aware assessment graded 77.7% of the 1,933 pairs lower than the database (76.3% of assessed pairs), including 352 from high, and low systemic absorption explained 80.3% of those downgrades. On the 118-case author-derived reference set, exact agreement was 0.856 under the original frozen labels (101/118; kappa 0.779) and 0.881 after three post-lock label corrections (104/118); on the 40-case frozen blind set it was 0.900 (36/40); the 20 B-only cases scored 18/20 frozen and 20/20 corrected (descriptive, not development-free). 10-token frozen-label agreement was 0.419-0.714. A second configuration changing model, token allowance and sampling gave 0.576-0.737 under corrected labels (rule_context row: pre-correction prompt), with high-risk sensitivity 0.214-0.536; the cascade's sensitivity held at 0.893. On a semi-independent 92-case audit, agreement was 0.674 but a high-risk sensitivity of 0.320 (8 of 25) under applied labels and 0.276 (8 of 29) re-derived, its principal limitation; on the 62 cases whose labels do not depend on its own absorption assumption, agreement was 0.548. A second-configuration baseline scored higher on the full audit set (0.728), confined to the 26 cases whose DDInter statement it retrieves; excluding them, 0.652 vs 0.712 for the cascade. Two clinicians agreed on 11 of 12 cases (kappa 0.840) yet both rated 8 of 12 lower (6 of 12 frozen)—descriptively, not as validation. Conclusions. Database severity, evidence-graded rules and clinician ratings assign ophthalmic DDI severity grades differently, and none of the three is a ground truth. An absorption-first deterministic cascade makes those disagreements enumerable, traceable and auditable, but its false-negative profile requires prospective, outcome-linked calibration.
