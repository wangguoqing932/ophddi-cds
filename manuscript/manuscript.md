## Abstract

**Background.** Ophthalmic topical medications reach clinically meaningful systemic exposure, yet general-purpose drug-drug interaction (DDI) databases grade severity from systemic administration and do not model absorption from the topical route. Whether that assumption holds at registry scale is unknown. **Methods.** We built OphDDI-CDS, a deterministic three-layer cascade (40 mechanism rules, each with a source record; an absorption-scaled class matrix; a hard-constraint layer) over 113 ophthalmic and 232 systemic agents, and compared three ways of assigning risk: systemic-route database severity, evidence-graded rules, and an exploratory clinician grade review. **Results.** Against DDInter severity for all 1,933 registry-matched pairs, 75.3% were graded lower once absorption was modelled (352 downgraded from high), and 80.5% of downgrades were attributable to low systemic absorption. On a 118-case literature-derived gold the cascade reached 0.907 exact accuracy (kappa 0.857) versus 0.415–0.737 for three LLM baselines. Under a 3000-token reasoning-model re-run, baseline agreement rose (0.576–0.737) but high-risk sensitivity fell to 0.214–0.536; the cascade retained 0.964. On a semi-independent 92-case audit set the cascade reached 0.674 accuracy with high-risk sensitivity 0.320 (8 of 25); on the 62 cases free of the system's own absorption assumption, 0.548 (34 of 62); excluding the 26 cases whose DDInter statement is retrievable, the adequate-budget retrieval baseline matches the no-retrieval baseline at 0.652 (43/66) against 0.712 (47/66) for the cascade. One adequate-budget baseline scored higher on the full set (0.728). In an exploratory grade review, two ophthalmologists agreed closely with each other (11 of 12; kappa 0.840) but rated most cases below the assigned grades; the exercise is descriptive and is not reported as validation. **Conclusions.** Systemic-route databases, evidence-graded rules and clinical intuition assign risk differently, and the first grades three of every four registry-matched pairs lower than absorption-aware assessment does, and differs on more than four in five. An absorption-first deterministic cascade makes those disagreements enumerable, traceable and auditable; its false-negative profile and clinical value require prospective outcome-linked calibration, and the literature-derived gold bounds all accuracy claims reported here.

## 1. Introduction

Ophthalmic topical medications are among the most widely prescribed drugs, yet their systemic absorption is frequently underestimated in drug-drug interaction (DDI) screening. Although eye drops are assumed to act locally, several agents reach clinically meaningful systemic exposure: timolol attains plasma concentrations comparable to low oral doses through nasolacrimal drainage (Alvan et al., 1980; Rait, 1999; Korte et al., 2002), topical carteolol can cause atrioventricular block (Yoshioka and Kawasaki, 2025), severe reactions following ophthalmic timolol have been linked to CYP2D6 poor metabolizers (Bollinger et al., 2023), and documented events include cardiogenic shock with topical timolol plus verapamil (Pringle and MacEwen, 1987) and case series of bradycardia and syncope (Rains and Kesterson, 2021; Abbas et al., 2020). Alpha-2 agonists cause central nervous system depression in children (Kiryazov et al., 2013; Rangan et al., 2008; Hoffmann et al., 2004; Gunes et al., 2016), and paediatric patients are generally more susceptible (Sacchi et al., 2020). Nasolacrimal occlusion reduces exposure (Xu et al., 2017), whereas in elderly patients on multiple systemic medications such additive effects can accumulate into harm (Diggory and Franks, 1997; Masnoon et al., 2017).

General-purpose DDI databases, including DDInter, publish pair-level severity ratings derived predominantly from systemic administration (Xiong et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2024). DDI screening is clinically relevant but methodologically heterogeneous (Percha and Altman, 2013), and automated detection remains incomplete (Yao et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2026). Crucially, these databases omit the decisive ophthalmic modifier — the magnitude of systemic absorption from topical administration — and the corresponding dose-route correction; contextualized algorithms exist for patient factors but not for route-specific absorption (Chou et al., 2021). Route awareness is not wholly absent (Drugs.com keeps a separate ophthalmic entity for timolol; Lexicomp asks for the route), but an explicit registry-wide absorption model is missing, and neither the size of the mismatch nor its relation to clinician judgment has been measured.

Large language models (LLMs) offer flexibility for DDI screening but raise reliability concerns: unconstrained models can hallucinate and are non-reproducible, retrieval-augmented variants inherit the biases of their corpora (Clusmann et al., 2026), and prospective evaluations of LLM-based decision support remain limited (Leibovitch et al., 2026). In our own evaluation, retrieval augmentation closed only part of the gap to a purpose-built cascade, and the ranking of retrieval methods depended on the output budget (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009; Guo et al., 2024; Section 3.6). Systematic reviews of computerized clinical decision support report modest and variable effects on practice (Kawamoto et al., 2005; Bright et al., 2012), and high alert-override rates remain a central adoption problem (van der Sijs et al., 2006; Edrees et al., 2020; Ray et al., 2026).

We therefore built OphDDI-CDS, a deterministic three-layer cascade — 40 mechanism rules each with a source record, an absorption-scaled drug-class matrix, and a hard-constraint layer — over 113 ophthalmic and 232 systemic agents, grounded in 1,307 approved, source-tagged evidence chunks. The production path makes no LLM call, shows zero variance across temperature conditions, and returns matched rule identifiers with every grade. Our contributions are fourfold. First, we quantify rather than assert the divergence: across all 1,933 ophthalmic–systemic pairs that carry a DDInter severity rating, absorption-aware assessment grades 75.3% lower, 352 pairs move down from high, and low systemic absorption from the topical route accounts for 80.5% of all downgrades. Second, we provide the instrument that makes the measurement possible, with every decision traceable to rule identifiers and source records. Third, we show that the divergence is not one disagreement but three, by examining the same direction of disagreement across three overlapping but non-identical pair sets: systemic-route severity, evidence-graded assessment, and a clinician grade review reported as a perspective comparison rather than as validation (Sections 2.5 and 3.8). Fourth, we report the evaluation in a form that can be audited and re-run, following TRIPOD+AI and DECIDE-AI reporting principles (Collins et al., 2024; Vasey et al., 2022), including the two reference sets on which the system agrees poorly.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Study design and data sources

Evaluation followed a four-phase protocol: rule consistency, development-phase validation, a semi-independent external audit against DDInter, and a two-expert clinical grade review — a perspective comparison rather than an independent assessment, since one rater is a member of the corresponding author's research team (Section 2.5). The system was compared with three LLM baselines under five temperature conditions.

Route is modelled through two mechanisms rather than a route field: every ophthalmic agent carries one of six systemic-absorption tiers, the proxy for how much of a topical dose reaches the circulation, and the rule layer encodes route-dependent predicates directly, for example a rule requiring the `systemic_absorption_high` flag before it fires. Route therefore gates which rules and matrix cells can assign a grade (tiers per agent in Supplementary Table S5).

Data sources were DDInter 2.0, a curated database of 302,516 DDI records (Tian et al., 2024); FDA prescribing labels for ophthalmic products; Stockley's Drug Interactions and the British National Formulary (Joint Formulary Committee, 2026); and PubMed. All 1,307 evidence chunks were filtered to approved-release status and graded by evidence level (A, regulatory label; B, meta-analysis or systematic review; C, original study; D, in vitro, animal, or pharmacokinetic first principles; E, class-level mechanism inference): A = 53, B = 191, C = 215, D = 584, E = 264.

### 2.2 System architecture and knowledge base

The production system (`full_system`) is a three-layer deterministic cascade with zero LLM calls (Figure 1).

**L1 rule engine.** Forty mechanism rules cover additive beta-blockade, beta-blocker–calcium-channel-blocker effects, additive alpha-2 CNS depression, CYP3A4 induction or inhibition, and QT prolongation with low-absorption fluoroquinolones. Each rule is an entity-flag predicate with match-all or match-any semantics, and the highest-severity hit is retained (MAX-MATCH). The 43 policy entries comprise 40 rules plus 3 patient-factor policies (Table 2). No second-order interactions are modelled: each rule is evaluated independently, and the only cross-layer composition is the final maximum of the two layers. Combination products are tiered by their dominant component.

Citation coverage is narrower than "citation-verified" implies. Every rule carries at least one source record, but the records are heterogeneous: 41 DrugBank entries, 7 regulatory label records, 2 DOIs, 2 DDInter pair annotations and 1 PubMed citation. Independent verification against Crossref, OpenAlex and PubMed with retraction screening covers 8 manifest items spanning 7 DOIs, of which three rules resolve to a verified literature citation; the rest cite DrugBank or label records not independently re-verified (Supplementary Text S2). Two consequences follow: "citation-verified" must not be read as covering every rule, and `beta_blocker_digoxin_additive` cites a study of additive topical-plus-systemic beta-blockade rather than a digoxin-specific source, grading as high a pair its own DDInter annotation records as moderate. We retain the conservative grade and flag the gap.

**L2 class matrix.** The configuration declares 21 ophthalmic row keys and 40 systemic column keys (34 used) and assigns a level to 104 cells: 83 actionable, 21 marked "none". Four upgrade rules require both a class match and specific absorption or selectivity flags. An absorption-scaling step sets agents with low, very-low, minimal or no absorption to low directly; upgrades are evaluated only for high or medium tiers, so no upgrade can raise a low-tier pair. That step is **not empirically validated** against outcome data: it is an author-specified decision grounded in the principle that a dose which does not reach the systemic circulation cannot produce a systemic interaction, and its behavioural consequence is quantified in Section 3.4. The matrix is sparse by design, and any pair reaching neither a rule nor a defined cell falls through to the default-low path.

**Which layer produces the grade.** Across all 26,216 registry pairs the class matrix determines 68.7%, the G19 constraint 12.2%, the rule layer 12.0% and an explicit default-low path 7.1%; the four paths are disjoint (Figure 1). Because the rule layer is not uniformly absorption-gated, 202 pairs whose ophthalmic agent carries a low-or-below tier receive medium (107) or high (95) grades, all through the rule path; every one of the 95 high grades comes from a single rule, `nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism` (Section 4.2).

**L3 hard constraint.** Low or very-low absorption with no rule hit and no matrix signal yields low risk (G19). Unknown or ambiguous medications are not silently classified: the engine returns an explicit unknown-drug path and the service raises an error requesting resolution, so out-of-coverage pairs are never assigned a grade by default.

**Absorption tiers.** Tiers were assigned from pharmacokinetic sources and regulatory labels, including nasolacrimal-drainage bioavailability and plasma levels for timolol (Alvan et al., 1980; Müller et al., 2020) and instillation-technique effects on exposure (Xu et al., 2017). The registry uses six values (113 agents: 15 high, 17 medium, 61 low, 9 very-low, 7 minimal, 4 none), and the L3 hard constraint tests two of these, `systemic_absorption_low` and `systemic_absorption_very_low`; the matrix layer already maps minimal and none to low. The tier is a **categorical assignment, not a measured quantity**: the intended reading is fractional systemic bioavailability after instillation, but sources report heterogeneous quantities, so the tiers are an explicitly stated ordinal model. Two limitations follow (Section 4.4): flurbiprofen was tiered high against a low curation-file record and is now low, and nine of the 113 agents are not topically administered (six intravitreal anti-VEGF agents, intraocular acetylcholine, two surgical viscoelastics). The source column does not evidence the tiers individually: 103 of 113 rows carry `DrugBank DBxxxxx|PubMed`, 9 carry `PubMed` alone and 1 carries `PubMed|Chinese FDA`, with individual sources named only for timolol (Alvan et al., 1980; Müller et al., 2020) and for atropine and dexamethasone (Xu et al., 2026); the rest are the study team's curation assignment, marked as such in Supplementary Table S5, and ocular drug transfer after systemic administration is itself incompletely characterized (Jumbe and Miller, 2021).

The evidence base was embedded with a 2048-dimensional Doubao model (`doubao-embedding-vision-251215`) and indexed with LightRAG (Guo et al., 2024): 1,307 documents, from which extraction produced 4,956 entities and 6,885 relations. Freshness was verified against evidence fingerprints. Retrieval baselines used BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) over the same chunks.

### 2.3 Validation datasets and evaluation protocol

**L1 literature-derived gold (118 cases).** The set combines three subsets with different provenance, recorded per case in `data/gold/l1_gold_provenance.csv`: 20 cases annotated by the study team from named literature sources, 58 guideline-sourced cases covering classes where the systemic and ophthalmic routes diverge most, and a procedurally frozen 40-case blind subset. Twenty labels (G01–G20, tagged `T1_literature_consensus`) were annotated from FDA labels, Stockley's, BNF tables and Drugs.com, with the supporting statement and encoded mechanism deposited for each; five (G04, G05, G08, G16, G19) differ from the DDInter severity for the same pair. The remaining 98 labels were annotated by the two authors of the original submission from the same categories of source; the two authors added during revision contributed clinical input but did not construct the gold. **How the 58 guideline labels were set.** Those cases were drawn from the evidence that specific rules cite, and for 47 of the 58 the gold label is the severity of that rule; the other 11 were overridden by hand after review (G113, G114, G125, G126, G128, G131–G134, G149, G154). Agreement on the 47 therefore measures rule fidelity by construction rather than independent agreement with the literature, and the manuscript reports the two groups separately. The 58 guideline labels and the initial 40 blind proposals were drafted without reference to system outputs, with one exception: on 31 July 2026, after the lock and before the questionnaire, B02 (high→medium), B12 (medium→high) and C17 (low→medium) were corrected to the engine's grades (timeline in Section 3.8, results reported both ways). The eleven remaining disagreements are listed with rationale in Supplementary Table S1. One blind half (C01–C20) entered the development loop; the other (B01–B20) was never used in engine development. Distribution: 28 high, 51 medium, 39 low (Table 4; per-dataset in Supplementary Table S8).

**DDInter audit set (92 cases).** A stratified random sample by absorption tier and DDInter severity. The stated correction rule was to set a case to low when the agent's tier is low or lower, no rule fires and no matrix upgrade applies. As applied, 30 cases were corrected and 62 kept, with three documented deviations: seven corrected cases are in fact reached by a rule; the two aflibercept pairs (`minimal_systemic_absorption`) were kept although the rule would correct them; and the four flurbiprofen pairs were kept on the superseded high tier recorded at the lock. Re-deriving the gold under the stated rule with current tiers changes 10 of 92 labels, giving 60 of 92 (0.652) with sensitivity 8 of 29, against 62 of 92 (0.674) and 8 of 25 under the applied gold. The audit is therefore semi-independent: the severity component is third-party, but the absorption correction shares the system's assumption.

**Reference sets.** A development set (200 DDInter-derived cases), an external PubMed co-mention set (70 cases) and an internal validation set (78 cases) served as development gates, not validation. The first two carry unadjusted systemic-route gold and are reported in Section 3.2 as a quantification of the adjustment rather than performance. The internal set overlaps 78 of the 118 L1 cases by identity with identical labels.

### 2.4 Baselines and evaluation protocol

Four methods were compared: `pure_llm` (one LLM call with a domain-general prompt and no retrieved evidence), `naive_rag` (BM25 retrieval plus LLM), `lightrag` (LLM with knowledge-graph context) and `full_system` (deterministic cascade). The three LLM methods shared one system prompt with the three-level risk definitions and a general absorption reminder (Supplementary Text S3).

`naive_rag` uses `rank_bm25.BM25Okapi` (k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75) over the same 1,307 chunks; the query is `f"{a} {b} ophthalmic interaction"`, with `a` and `b` the ophthalmic and systemic drug names, so the query includes the drug names, and the top-3 passages are retained (top-k = 3), each truncated to 400 characters. `lightrag` prepends a `Drug profiles:` block rendered by `PreciseKG.full_kg_context()`, which reads the registries and rule configuration rather than querying the vector index, so this is a knowledge-graph context baseline rather than a vector-retrieval baseline. That block contains the drug profiles, the matched rule identifiers with their severities, and — for low-absorption pairs with no matched rule — an explicit instruction that the grade must be low. The baseline therefore receives part of the rule layer, so the gap between it and `full_system` understates what retrieval augmentation would achieve without those hints. LLM baselines used the DeepSeek identifier `deepseek-chat` with a 10-token cap and no chain-of-thought prompting; the endpoint exposes neither parameter count nor checkpoint revision. Input was English, with temperature 0.0 for condition 0 and 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.2 for conditions 1–4. Outputs were parsed by substring matching with an explicit unknown fallback; one of the 9,600 primary predictions returned an unparseable string and was scored as unknown. Because the 1,307-chunk corpus also informed the gold labels, the retrieval baselines access gold-source material the deterministic system does not use at inference, an asymmetry that gives the baselines, if anything, an advantage. Five conditions × four methods × four datasets produced 9,600 prediction rows with zero API failures; run configuration and file-level fingerprints are in Supplementary Text S5.

Table 3 reports two output-budget settings: **10-token** (`deepseek-chat`, one call per case, 10-token cap, mean over five temperature conditions) and **3000-token** (reasoning model `glm-5.3-flash`, 3000-token budget, temperature 0.0, majority vote over five repeated samples). The reasoning-model re-run used the Zhipu coding-plan endpoint, with reasoning tokens counted against the budget; reasoning effort was not set, so the endpoint default applied. No response was truncated and no split vote arose, since in every case at least three of five samples returned the same label. The run produced 3,150 raw responses. Because it changes model, budget and sampling together, it is reported as a second setting rather than as an isolated test of reasoning.

Metrics were exact three-level accuracy (Acc3), high-risk sensitivity and specificity (high versus non-high), high-risk F1, and Cohen's kappa (Cohen, 1960) with Landis–Koch interpretation (Landis and Koch, 1977). Method differences were tested with the exact two-sided McNemar test on discordant pairs at condition 0. Confidence intervals are Wilson for proportions, Clopper–Pearson for perfect subsets, and nonparametric bootstrap (2,000 resamples) for kappa. No power calculation was performed; all intervals are descriptive.

### 2.5 Expert grade review

Two ophthalmologists reviewed 12 cases under a grade-review protocol (Supplementary Table S3). Both are clinicians of the Department of Ophthalmology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, and one is a member of the corresponding author's research team, so we use "perspective comparison" rather than "independent assessment". For each case the instrument displayed the drug pair, a short scenario, the proposed gold grade and a system grade with a one-line rationale; the expert recorded their own judgment on the same three-level scale. Because both grades were visible, this is a grade review and not a blinded assessment. Cases spanned errata confirmations, upgrade disputes, downgrade confirmations, potency-granularity boundaries and typical cases, and were constructed around the system's outputs, which is why the exercise cannot serve as independent validation; mechanism-level evidence was not provided. Ratings were collected on paper on 3 August 2026 and transcribed on 8 August; expert 1's questionnaire is deposited as a scan and both sets as transcribed records. The questionnaire asked for the expert's independent judgment but did not describe research use or publication. An earlier 12-item instrument prepared on 30 July 2026 was never administered and is retained only as a process record. Agreement is summarized with Cohen's kappa, unweighted and quadratic-weighted on the ordinal scale low < medium < high, as descriptive summaries of a 12-case purposively selected panel.

### 2.6 Intended use and regulatory status

The system is a research-grade, mechanism-based second reader for clinicians and pharmacists screening ophthalmic–systemic combinations. It is not intended to replace clinical or pharmacy judgment, out-of-coverage medications are rejected with an explicit error rather than scored, and the web interface carries a disclaimer to this effect. It is not marketed as a medical device; prospective clinical deployment would require formal regulatory assessment.

### 2.7 Software and reproducibility

The system is implemented in Python 3.11 (package `ophthalmic_ddi_cds_agent`). Source code, the knowledge base, citation-verification tooling and evaluation scripts are publicly available at https://github.com/wangguoqing932/ophddi-cds (release tag `v3.0-frontiers`), with a synthetic demonstration dataset and a one-command entry point (`python scripts/demo_pipeline.py`, 25 mock pairs). Engine, rules and gold versions are pinned by SHA-256 fingerprints over LF-normalised content, because a digest otherwise depends on line endings; the repository-root `MANIFEST.sha256` lists them so any checkout can be compared against the evaluated version. The report follows TRIPOD+AI guidance where applicable (Collins et al., 2024), with a checklist as Supplementary Text S6, and items that do not apply to a rule-based tool are marked. Generative AI use is documented in the Declarations: DeepSeek-chat for baseline evaluations and as an assistive tool in registry curation, evidence grading and gold-label annotation, and Zhipu GLM-4.6V for visual verification of scanned questionnaires. No AI tool is an author, and all AI-assisted content was reviewed by the named authors.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 System construction

The knowledge base comprises 113 ophthalmic agents (104 topically administered; 9 intravitreal, intraocular or surgical) and 232 systemic medications (Table 1; machine-readable form in Supplementary Table S4), 1,307 approved evidence chunks (A = 53, B = 191, C = 215, D = 584, E = 264; Supplementary Text S1) and a LightRAG index over 1,307 documents. The rule engine comprises 40 mechanism rules plus 3 patient-factor policies (43 policy entries), each with at least one source record and zero policy errors (Table 2; full inventory in Supplementary Table S6). The class matrix defines 104 cells across 21 ophthalmic row keys and 34 used systemic columns. Figure 4 illustrates representative outputs for the demonstration cases.

### 3.2 Development-phase and gold-calibration reference results

The engine achieved 100% rule consistency on the development dataset. On the internal validation set (78 cases) sensitivity was 1.000 and specificity 0.984; we report this as a development gate, not as independent evidence, because 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap it by case identity with identical labels. Against the unadjusted systemic-route gold of the development set (200 cases) the system achieved 0.210 exact accuracy (sensitivity 0.069, specificity 0.895); against the external PubMed co-mention gold (70 cases, audited as noisy) it achieved 0.443 (sensitivity 0.077, specificity 0.935). Under the evaluation protocol these low values are expected: they quantify the magnitude of the absorption correction relative to systemic-route labels and are not interpretable as performance.

### 3.3 Agreement with the literature-derived gold (118 cases)

On this set the gold and the rules draw on the same literature, so agreement measures the internal consistency of an evidence-graded encoding rather than accuracy against an independent standard; what it supports is a like-for-like comparison of four methods on identical cases (Table 3, Figure 2; per-dataset detail in Supplementary Table S7).

`full_system` achieved 0.907 exact accuracy (107/118; Wilson 95% CI 0.841–0.947), 0.964 high-risk sensitivity (27 of 28; CI 0.823–0.994), 0.989 specificity and kappa 0.857 (bootstrap CI 0.772–0.934), with zero variance across conditions. `lightrag` reached 0.737 accuracy, 0.779 sensitivity and 0.936 specificity (kappa 0.603); `pure_llm` 0.475 and 0.771 (kappa 0.256); `naive_rag` 0.415 and 0.757 (kappa 0.182). Exact McNemar tests at condition 0 were significant against each baseline: versus `pure_llm` 54 discordant pairs in favour of the system to 2 against (p = 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁴), versus `naive_rag` 60 to 4 (p = 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁴), versus `lightrag` 20 to 1 (p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁵). The gradient `full_system` > `lightrag` > `pure_llm` > `naive_rag` held across all five temperature conditions, and the 0.170 gap to the best baseline is the margin over a comparator that already receives the matched rules (Section 2.4), so it should not be read as the contribution of retrieval augmentation alone. Per subset, the frozen blind cases reached 39 of 40, the 20-case mechanism subset 20 of 20 and the guideline subset 48 of 58; eleven exact-scale disagreements remain, each with a documented rationale (Supplementary Table S1). Decomposing the guideline subset as described in Section 2.3, the system agrees on 43 of the 47 cases whose label equals the cited rule's severity and on 5 of the 11 hand-overridden cases; the 0.907 headline is therefore a weighted average of rule fidelity (43/47), agreement with hand-adjudicated labels (5/11) and agreement on the 60 T1 and frozen cases (59/60), and should not be read as a single independent accuracy estimate.

### 3.4 Registry-scale divergence

This result does not depend on the gold labels, since both sides are pre-specified: DDInter supplies a systemic-route severity rating and the cascade an absorption-aware grade. We compared every ophthalmic–systemic pair for which DDInter 2.0 provides a severity record — 1,933 pairs. Of these, 1,455 (75.3%) were graded lower by the system, including 352 downgraded from high (37 to medium, 315 to low; 18.2% of all pairs) and 1,103 from medium to low; 136 (7.0%) were upgraded, so the two perspectives differ for 82.3% of pairs. Of all downgrades, 80.5% (1,171 of 1,455) were attributable to low systemic absorption. In other words, three of every four rated pairs would reach a prescriber at a different grade under absorption-first assessment, and most of that shift follows from a single pharmacokinetic mechanism rather than from many independent judgements.

Two caveats bound this measurement. It quantifies divergence, not error: the two perspectives rarely coincide for the topical route, but that does not make the database ratings wrong. And the tiers driving the downgrades were assigned by the study team from pharmacokinetic sources and labels (Section 2.2), so the measurement is a property of an explicitly stated absorption model rather than an independent pharmacokinetic measurement.

### 3.5 Semi-independent DDInter audit (92 cases)

On the audit set `full_system` reached 0.674 exact accuracy (62/92; Wilson CI 0.573–0.761) and kappa 0.428 against the absorption-corrected gold (specificity 0.955). Only part of that set is independent of the system's own assumption: restricting to the 62 cases kept at third-party DDInter severity gives 34 of 62 (0.548), the figure free of that assumption, which should be read as the audit headline rather than 0.674. Against uncorrected DDInter labels agreement was 0.370 (34/92). Of the 30 corrected cases the system agreed on 28 (93%), and of the 62 kept cases on 34 (55%) (Table 5). Baselines on the corrected gold were `pure_llm` 0.630, `lightrag` 0.591 and `naive_rag` 0.535. McNemar tests were significant versus `lightrag` (8 versus 1; p = 0.039) but not versus `naive_rag` (27 versus 17; p = 0.174) or `pure_llm` (18 versus 15; p = 0.728). Symmetrically, `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` exceed the system on sensitivity (0.848 each versus 0.320; `lightrag` is lower at 0.264), so the cascade's advantage lies in specificity and exact accuracy, not sensitivity.

This comparison is partly confounded. Of the 1,307 chunks, 500 are DDInter severity statements and the audit gold for the 62 third-party cases is the DDInter value itself; the top-3 BM25 passages contain the pair's own statement for 19 of those 62, so `naive_rag` retrieves the gold label there, with a weaker version of the same effect for `lightrag`, while the cascade does not query that corpus at inference. With the 26 retrievable cases removed, adequate-budget `naive_rag` is correct on 43 of the remaining 66 (0.652), identical to `pure_llm`, against 47 of 66 (0.712) for the cascade, and its sensitivity falls from 9 of 10 to 1 of 15. Under the re-derived gold the cascade scores 60 of 92 (0.652), so the conclusions hold under either reading.

High-risk sensitivity on the audit set was 0.320 (8 of 25 gold-high cases; CI 0.172–0.516), above `lightrag` (0.264) but well below `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` (0.848 each). That this figure is low in absolute terms is the principal limitation of the current system, and we report it deliberately. The 30 disagreements were directionally consistent with the absorption-first design: 17 gold-high pairs were downgraded (8 to low, 9 to medium), 9 gold-medium pairs to low, 2 gold-medium pairs upgraded to high, and 1 gold-low pair each to medium and high (Supplementary Table S2). What the system could miss is concentrated in clinically sensitive pairs: betaxolol with verapamil or diltiazem, timolol with epinephrine, anticholinergic combinations with topiramate, and flurbiprofen with methotrexate. The last illustrates how the absorption model interacts with third-party labels: flurbiprofen is formulated at 0.03%, the lowest concentration of any ophthalmic NSAID in the registry, so correcting its tier moved this pair and three related anticoagulant pairs further from the DDInter gold rather than closer. That divergence is between a systemic-route label and a low-absorption agent, not between a matrix cell and its gold, and for a screening tool this false-negative profile is the clinically critical number.

### 3.6 Reasoning-model baselines under an adequate output budget

Because the primary comparison used a non-reasoning model with a 10-token cap, we repeated the three baselines under conditions that remove that constraint, reporting both settings. The re-run used a reasoning model, a 3000-token budget, temperature 0.0 with five repeated samples and a pre-specified parsing rule; prompt, retrieval settings and templates were unchanged. All 3,150 calls completed with no errors and no unparseable responses.

Label agreement improved. On the 118-case set `pure_llm` rose from 0.475 to 0.576 and `naive_rag` from 0.415 to 0.585, while `lightrag` was unchanged at 0.737. High-risk sensitivity, however, fell sharply and specificity rose to approximately 1.000 (Table 3): sensitivity moved from 0.771/0.757/0.779 to 0.250/0.214/0.536 for `pure_llm`, `naive_rag` and `lightrag`, while the cascade held at 0.964 with specificity 0.989. The mechanism is a shift in the predicted label distribution rather than better discrimination: under the larger budget `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` assign high risk to 6% of cases and `lightrag` to 13%, where the gold contains 24%, and on the audit set to 2% where the gold contains 27%. Because both gold sets are dominated by low and medium grades, this conservatism inflates exact accuracy while the baselines identify only 4 to 15 of the 28 gold-high cases across repeats, or 7 to 15 under majority vote, against 27 for the cascade. We therefore read the accuracy increase as an artefact of the decision rule adopted with more room, not as evidence that reasoning improves ophthalmic DDI screening; the sensitivity column is the informative one.

Two further results bear on the primary comparison. First, the ordering between retrieval methods reverses: under the 10-token cap `naive_rag` scored below `pure_llm`, whereas with an adequate budget it scores at or above it on both sets (0.585 versus 0.576; 0.728 versus 0.609), so the 10-token ordering is a property of the constrained setting. Second, on the audit set the adequate-budget `naive_rag` reaches 0.728 exact accuracy and 0.400 sensitivity, both above the cascade; that advantage is confined to cases whose own DDInter statement the retriever can reach, and with those 26 cases excluded it falls to 43 of 66 (0.652), below the cascade's 47 of 66 (0.712), with sensitivity falling from 9 of 10 to 1 of 15. Its sensitivity is also unstable across repeats (0.280 to 0.480), whereas the cascade returns the same grade every time. At a fixed temperature of 0.0 the baselines still varied by 0.013 to 0.034 in exact accuracy across repeats, which is why the reported figures use a majority vote.

### 3.7 Determinism and temperature sensitivity

`full_system` is deterministic: all five temperature conditions returned identical predictions (SD = 0.000 for every metric), a determinism check rather than a robustness claim. Figure 5 shows the per-layer latency trace for the worked example, in which the cascade completes in under 2 ms with no model call. The LLM baselines varied across conditions on the L1 set: `lightrag` accuracy between 0.712 and 0.771 (SD 0.020), `pure_llm` between 0.449 and 0.517 (SD 0.024) and `naive_rag` between 0.390 and 0.432 (SD 0.014), with a similar pattern on the audit set (SD 0.005–0.039). Because the conditions differ only in temperature, these standard deviations quantify sampling instability rather than seed variance.

### 3.8 Exploratory clinician grade review (12 cases)

Two ophthalmologists rated 12 cases with the proposed gold and a displayed system grade both visible (Figure 3; Section 2.5). Inter-rater agreement was 11 of 12 (kappa 0.840, 95% bootstrap CI 0.526–1.000; quadratic-weighted 0.875). Against the displayed grades, expert 1 agreed on 2 of 12 cases (kappa −0.176) and expert 2 on 1 of 12 (kappa −0.257); against the proposed gold, expert 1 on 4 of 12 (kappa 0.020) and expert 2 on 3 of 12 (kappa −0.091). The comparisons differ because for R10 and R11 the displayed grade was not the engine's grade. Against the gold each expert rated 8 of 12 cases lower (discordant judgments 8 of 8 and 8 of 9; R08 is expert 2's only higher rating), and neither rated any of the four displayed-high cases as high. Quadratic-weighted kappas are 0.473 and 0.438 against the gold and 0.355 and 0.313 against the displayed grades, placing the divergence at roughly one tier rather than at random disagreement; the maximum attainable unweighted kappa given the marginals is 0.294 and 0.314, so the low unweighted values partly reflect margin incompatibility.

Across all twelve cases the proposed gold equals the deposited engine grade, so the expert-versus-gold and expert-versus-displayed comparisons cannot separate the gold from the engine; the exercise is a comparison against the assigned grade, not a test of either. For ten of the twelve cases the displayed grade equals the deposited re-run. For R10 and R11 the questionnaire displayed medium, but that value was a class-level conservative hint written by the study team, not the engine's output: the engine grades both pairs low, matching the gold. No rule or matrix level has changed since the review and none of the reviewed pairs involves flurbiprofen. All twelve cases correspond to L1 cases; five belong to the frozen blind subset (R01 = B02, R02 = B12, R03 = C17, R10 = B18, R11 = C20) and the other seven are B06, C02, C03, B19, C10, C11 and G08. The blind sets were locked on 31 July 2026, three labels were corrected to the engine's grades the same day (B02 high→medium, B12 medium→high, C17 low→medium) and committed on 3 August, and the questionnaires were completed on 3 August; reporting both ways gives 39 of 40 and 107 of 118 under the corrected labels and 36 of 40 and 104 of 118 under the pre-correction labels.

The exercise is not a validation: both grades were visible, one expert is a study-team member, the cases were constructed around the system's outputs, and the sample is purposive. Case-level ratings are in Supplementary Table S3. What it supports is a perspective gap between clinical prior and evidence-based grading, not an error rate.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

Three perspectives on ophthalmic–systemic risk — systemic-route database severity, evidence-graded rules and prescribing clinicians — assign grades differently, and this study measures that difference on overlapping pair sets. Absorption-aware assessment grades three quarters of the registry-matched pairs lower than the database and a further 7.0% higher, so the two differ for more than four pairs in five, and the downgrades are dominated by low topical systemic absorption. Evidence-graded rules and clinical judgment also diverge: the two ophthalmologists agreed closely with each other (kappa 0.840) yet rated most cases one tier below the assigned grades (Section 3.8). Because the panel was not independent and the cases were built around the system's outputs, this is a perspective comparison rather than a validation. Three observations make a perspective difference more plausible than an error: clinically serious events from ophthalmic-systemic DDI are rare, so expert judgment rationally gravitates toward lower tiers; the interactions clinicians recall are dominated by non-selective beta-blockers, so systematic coverage is most valuable where recall is weakest; and reports of serious adverse events are sparse, although pharmacovigilance analyses of ophthalmic agents do identify systemic signals (Xu et al., 2026; Prasad and Ramsey, 2026). High alert-override rates (van der Sijs et al., 2006) further suggest that a tool sitting one tier above clinical intuition will be treated as noise unless its justifications are transparent, which is what the traceable decision paths provide. The results therefore support absorption-first knowledge engineering as a way of making ophthalmic DDI risk explicit and individually auditable, and they are consistent with the absence of a shared grading standard for this route rather than with error in any one tool.

The quantitative results bound that claim rather than carry it. The cascade reached 0.907 exact accuracy on the 118-case gold (107/118) and outperformed three LLM baselines (0.415–0.737) with significant McNemar tests and zero variance across temperature conditions. On the audit set and within the primary protocol it led on exact accuracy (0.674 versus 0.535–0.630) and specificity (0.955), but its high-risk sensitivity was low in absolute terms (0.320, 8 of 25), below `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` (0.848 each) though above `lightrag` (0.264), and its advantage over those two was not significant; under the adequate-budget re-run one baseline exceeded it, and with retrievable-statement cases excluded that advantage disappears. Because the L1 gold shares its literature sources with the rules, and 47 of its 58 guideline labels are the cited rule's own severity, 0.907 is best read as the internal consistency of an evidence-graded encoding rather than independent accuracy; the audit sensitivity bounds what the system can claim as a safety screen today.

### 4.2 What we add relative to prior work

Against general-purpose DDI databases, our system makes the ophthalmic absorption factor an explicit modelling component: a tier on every ophthalmic agent, an absorption-scaling step inside the matrix layer, and a hard constraint, with the final grade the maximum of the rule and matrix layers. That maximum is the honest limitation of the design. Because the rule layer is not uniformly gated, 202 pairs with a low-or-below tier receive medium (107) or high (95) grades through rules that require no absorption flag, all 95 high grades from a single rule. Gating that rule would lower agreement with the L1 gold from 0.907 to 0.890, and gating all four NSAID rules to 0.847. We report the behaviour rather than re-engineer it to fit the gold, and note that the gold's preference for an ungated rule is itself informative, because part of it was labelled from sources that reason about systemic exposure and so encodes the assumption this paper argues against. Whether to gate these rules is a calibration decision for outcome-linked evidence, not gold agreement.

Against LLM and retrieval-augmented approaches, the practical difference is not label agreement alone — which favours the cascade on L1 and the full audit set but not on the label-independent subset — but the sensitivity–specificity profile and the reproducibility of the decision. Machine-learning predictors built on molecular structure (Ryu et al., 2018) or knowledge-graph representations (Lin et al., 2026) target systemic-route interactions and likewise omit route-specific absorption. In our evaluation the graph-context baseline captured only part of the gain although all LLM baselines received the same absorption reminder, and under an adequate budget the baselines converged on a conservative policy. The cascade is deterministic by construction, so reproducibility is a design property rather than a robustness result, and every decision returns matched rule IDs, a decision path and the rule's source record where available.

The largest methodological implication is that a severity rating exported from a systemic-route database is not a valid ophthalmic ground truth, and any evaluation treating it as one measures the distance between two assumptions rather than the tool's accuracy. That is why we report agreement with unadjusted systemic-route labels (0.210 and 0.443, Section 3.2) as a quantification of the adjustment rather than as performance.

### 4.4 Limitations

**Absorption-tier assignment.** The tiers are the most load-bearing variable and the least independently documented: a study-team assignment with no operational measurement behind every agent, with one defect corrected during revision and one open. Flurbiprofen was tiered high against a low curation-file record and is now low. Thirteen further agents show the same registry-versus-curation discrepancy; we adopt the registry as authoritative because the engine loads it at inference, and re-running the 1,933-pair audit under the curation-file tiers moves the downgrade share from 75.27% to 75.69% and leaves the from-high downgrades at 352, so the conclusion is robust to that choice. Nine of the 113 agents are not topical, including aflibercept, so the registry-scale divergence is a property of this stated absorption model rather than an independent pharmacokinetic measurement.

**Bounded clinical impact.** The scarcity of direct evidence admits two explanations: clinically detectable systemic effects may be uncommon, or publication and ascertainment bias may operate; the present data cannot distinguish them. The evidence base rests largely on pharmacokinetic first principles, class-level pharmacology and extrapolation from systemic data, with few ophthalmic-specific outcome studies. If the true risk gradient clusters at the low end, an evidence-graded system may over-grade relative to practice; conversely, audit sensitivity of 0.320 shows it can under-grade. At an illustrative 1% prevalence the audit operating point (sensitivity 0.320, specificity 0.955) yields a positive predictive value of about 6.7%, so the system's value lies in systematic coverage and traceability rather than screening yield.

**Validation.** The L1 gold is heterogeneous: 20 of its 118 labels are study-team literature annotations, the remainder was author-annotated, and it is a literature-derived proxy rather than an independently adjudicated standard. Only the 40 frozen cases are procedurally independent, only 20 of those were free of development exposure, and three of their labels were corrected toward the engine's grades after the lock, so the subset is reported both ways (Section 3.8). In addition, 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap the development set with identical labels. The two-expert review is the only human channel beyond the annotation exercise and cannot serve as an independent test (Section 3.8). Headline results reflect the final engine and gold; earlier versions scored higher on the same cases (0.992), and the drift is disclosed in the process audit.

### 4.5 Future directions

Four directions follow, in order of precedence. **Registry correction:** every tier should be re-assigned with a documented source and threshold, and the nine non-topical agents reclassified or excluded, after which the registry-scale analysis should be re-run from the deposited tier table and scenario analysis; the same item covers whether `nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism` and the other NSAID rules should be gated on absorption. Second, **clinical calibration:** a larger expert panel reviewing mechanism-level evidence, or outcome-linked retrospective data, could determine whether a systematic one-tier offset applies to specific tiers or classes, with the audit false-negative pairs as first targets. Third, **false-negative surveillance:** targeted pharmacovigilance mining and prospective monitoring of high-absorption agents would test mechanism-derived rules against outcomes. Fourth, **coverage and scope:** extension of the registry, a user-visible unassessed status for out-of-coverage pairs, and transfer of the class-matrix architecture to dermatologic, inhaled and intranasal domains.

## 5. Conclusions

We developed OphDDI-CDS, an absorption-first deterministic cascade for ophthalmic–systemic DDI screening, and used it to compare three ways of assigning risk. Against DDInter severity, absorption-aware assessment graded 75.3% of all 1,933 registry-matched pairs lower, with 80.5% of that shift attributable to low topical systemic absorption; an exploratory grade review likewise found one-directional disagreement with the assigned grades, reported as a perspective gap rather than an error rate (Section 3.8). The cascade reached 0.907 exact accuracy on the 118-case gold (107/118; kappa 0.857) with complete output traceability, outperformed three LLM baselines on exact accuracy in the primary protocol, and on a semi-independent audit set reached 0.674 exact accuracy — falling to 0.548 on the subset free of its own absorption assumption — but a high-risk sensitivity of only 0.320, below `pure_llm` and `naive_rag` (0.848 each). That sensitivity, together with a literature-derived gold that is part study-team and part author annotation, bounds what the system can claim today. The practical conclusion is that ophthalmic DDI risk lacks a shared grading standard: database severity, evidence-graded rules and clinical prior disagree directionally in every sample studied, and evaluation here should measure that disagreement rather than assume any one is correct. Prospective, outcome-linked calibration is the next step.
