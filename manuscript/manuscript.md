## Abstract


**Background.** Ophthalmic topical medications reach clinically meaningful systemic exposure, yet general-purpose drug-drug interaction (DDI) databases grade severity from systemic administration and do not model absorption from the topical route. Whether that assumption holds at registry scale, and whether evidence-graded assessment agrees with prescribing clinicians, is unknown.

**Results.** We built OphDDI-CDS, a deterministic three-layer cascade (40 mechanism rules, each with a source record; an absorption-scaled class matrix; a hard-constraint layer) over 113 ophthalmic and 232 systemic agents, and compared three ways of assigning risk. Against DDInter severity for all 1,933 registry-matched pairs, 75.3% were graded lower once absorption was modelled (352 downgraded from high), and 80.5% of downgrades were attributable to low systemic absorption. On a 118-case literature-derived gold the cascade reached 0.907 exact accuracy (kappa 0.857) versus 0.415–0.737 for three LLM baselines. Under a 3000-token reasoning-model re-run, baseline agreement rose (0.576-0.737) but high-risk sensitivity fell to 0.214-0.536; the cascade retained 0.964. On a semi-independent 92-case audit set the cascade reached 0.674 accuracy with high-risk sensitivity 0.320 (8 of 25); on the 62 cases free of the system's own absorption assumption, 0.548 (34 of 62); excluding the 26 cases whose DDInter statement is retrievable, the adequate-budget retrieval baseline matches the no-retrieval baseline at 0.652 (43/66) against 0.712 (47/66) for the cascade. One adequate-budget baseline scored higher on the full set (0.728). In a descriptive clinician survey, two ophthalmologists — one a study-team member — reviewed 12 grades with the gold and a displayed system grade visible; they agreed closely with each other (11 of 12; kappa 0.840) but concurred with the gold on only 4/12 and 3/12 cases, rating below it in nearly all discordant cases. This is reported as a perspective comparison, not as validation.

**Conclusions.** The same ophthalmic–systemic pair is assigned different risk by systemic-route databases, evidence-graded rules and clinical intuition, and the first of these disagrees with absorption-aware assessment for three of every four registry-matched pairs. An absorption-first deterministic cascade makes those disagreements enumerable, traceable and auditable; its false-negative profile and clinical value require prospective outcome-linked calibration, and the literature-derived gold bounds all accuracy claims reported here.

## 1. Background

Ophthalmic topical medications are among the most widely prescribed drugs worldwide, yet their systemic absorption is frequently underestimated in routine DDI screening. Although eye drops are assumed to act locally, several agents achieve clinically meaningful systemic exposure. Non-selective beta-blockers such as timolol reach plasma concentrations comparable to low oral doses through nasolacrimal drainage and mucosal absorption; Alvan et al. measured plasma concentrations after ocular administration [1], and Rait notes that systemic absorption occurs in relatively small amounts but is clinically consequential in susceptible patients [2]. Korte et al. directly measured the systemic bioavailability and cardiopulmonary effects of 0.5% timolol eyedrops, the measurement that motivates route-specific interaction assessment [41]. Topical carteolol can likewise cause atrioventricular block [3], and severe systemic reactions to ophthalmic timolol have been linked to CYP2D6 poor metabolizers [4]. Severe systemic events are documented: topical timolol combined with verapamil can cause bradycardia and cardiogenic shock [5], and further case series of timolol-associated syncope have been reported [6,7]. Ageing patients are particularly vulnerable, including respiratory and cardiac complications of ocular beta-blockade [8]. Alpha-2 agonists can produce CNS depression, particularly in children [9-12], and pediatric patients are generally more susceptible to systemic effects of ocular medications [13]. Nasolacrimal occlusion or punctal compression measurably reduces the systemic exposure from instilled drops [14]. In elderly patients, who are often prescribed multiple systemic medications, such additive effects can accumulate into clinically significant harm [6-8,15].

General-purpose DDI databases, including DDInter, provide comprehensive pair-level severity ratings derived predominantly from systemic administration [16]. These resources are of limited direct utility for ophthalmic prescribing. DDI screening methods have long been recognized as clinically relevant but methodologically heterogeneous [17], and automated detection in practice remains incomplete [18,19]. Most importantly, general-purpose databases do not encode the decisive ophthalmic pharmacokinetic modifier, namely the magnitude of systemic absorption from topical administration, and they omit dose-route corrections derived from ophthalmic pharmacology. Route awareness is not wholly absent from mainstream resources: Drugs.com maintains a separate ophthalmic entity for timolol with its own interaction list and severity distribution, and Lexicomp asks the user to specify the route of administration. What is absent is not route awareness as such but an explicit, registry-wide absorption model that would let a prescriber see how a systemic-route severity rating changes once topical absorption is taken into account; that is the gap this work addresses. As a result, the same drug pair can be rated high-risk for oral administration while being clinically negligible for topical use. The magnitude of this mismatch has not been measured. It is acknowledged qualitatively that route matters, but no study has quantified, at registry scale, how often a systemic-route severity rating changes once absorption from the topical route is modelled; nor has an evidence-graded ophthalmic assessment been compared with the judgment of prescribing clinicians for the same pairs. Clinicians therefore lack both a mechanism-level ophthalmic-specific screening tool and any estimate of how far the interaction alerts they already receive depart from ophthalmic pharmacology.

Recent LLM approaches to clinical DDI screening offer flexibility but raise reliability concerns. Unconstrained models can hallucinate interactions and are non-reproducible, and retrieval-augmented variants inherit the biases of their retrieval corpora. In our own evaluation, retrieval-augmented variants captured only part of the gap to a purpose-built cascade, and the ranking of retrieval methods depended on how much output budget the model was given [20,21]; Section 3.6 reports both settings. Systematic reviews of computerized clinical decision support show modest but variable effects on practice [22,23], and high alert-override rates remain a central adoption problem [24,25]. Moreover, LLM outputs are non-deterministic and difficult to audit in a clinical context.

We present an ophthalmic DDI decision-support system built on a deterministic, fully explainable three-layer cascade. The L1 rule engine contains 40 mechanism rules, each carrying at least one source record. The L2 drug-class matrix applies absorption-scaled upgrades. The L3 hard constraint encodes ophthalmic systemic-absorption pharmacology. The system is grounded in a curated knowledge base of 1,307 verified evidence chunks (113 ophthalmic agents, 232 systemic agents). It produces traceable risk grades (high, medium, or low) with matched rule IDs and evidence chains. The production path requires zero LLM calls, shows zero variance across temperature conditions, and completes inference in milliseconds.

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we quantify the divergence rather than assert it: across all 1,933 ophthalmic–systemic pairs for which DDInter provides a severity rating, absorption-aware assessment grades 75.3% of pairs lower, 352 pairs move down from high, and a single nameable mechanism — low systemic absorption from the topical route — accounts for 80.5% of all downgrades. This turns a known qualitative caveat into a measured property of the database. Second, we provide the instrument that makes the measurement possible: a reproducible, source-traceable rule cascade for ophthalmic DDIs, including the class-matrix cells and absorption scaling absent from general-purpose databases, whose every decision returns the rule identifiers and evidence chain behind it. Third, we show that the divergence is not one disagreement but three, by comparing the same pairs under systemic-route severity, evidence-graded assessment, and expert grade review; on the 12-case clinician survey inter-rater agreement was 11 of 12 (kappa 0.840), yet each expert judged below the gold in 8 of 12 cases; because the case set was constructed around the system's outputs and one participant is a study-team member, the survey is reported as a descriptive perspective comparison rather than as validation. Fourth, we report the evaluation in a form that can be audited and re-run: a four-phase protocol (rule consistency, development-phase validation, a semi-independent external audit, and a clinical grade review (not an independent validation; see Section 4.4)) with all four datasets reported, including the two on which the system performs poorly, and a downloadable zero-configuration web interface for clinical and educational use. The system is positioned as a mechanism-based second reader, not as a replacement for clinical judgment.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Study design and data sources

We developed and evaluated a clinical decision-support system for DDIs between ophthalmic topical medications and systemic drugs. The system combines a deterministic, fully explainable rule cascade with a curated ophthalmic DDI knowledge base. Evaluation followed a four-phase protocol: rule consistency, development-phase validation, a semi-independent external audit against DDInter, and a two-expert clinical grade review — a perspective comparison rather than an independent assessment, since one of the two experts is a member of the study team (Section 2.5; Ethics declaration) — comparing the system with three LLM baselines under five temperature conditions.

**How route of administration is represented.** Route is modelled explicitly, through two mechanisms rather than through a route field. First, the ophthalmic registry is restricted to agents administered to the eye and each carries a systemic-absorption tier (high, medium, low, very-low, minimal or none); that tier is the operational proxy for how much of a topical dose reaches the systemic circulation, and it is what the absorption-scaling step acts on. Second, the rule layer encodes route-dependent predicates directly, for example a rule that requires the flag `systemic_absorption_high` before it fires. Route is therefore not a free-text attribute of a pair but a numeric modifier and a flag that gate which rules and matrix cells can assign a grade. The tier assignment procedure and its sources are described in Section 2.2 and listed per agent in Supplementary Table S5.

Evidence and gold labels were derived from multiple sources. DDInter 2.0, a curated database of 302,516 DDI records with expanded data coverage and new interaction types [42]. FDA prescribing labels for ophthalmic products provided product-specific warnings. Stockley's Drug Interactions and the British National Formulary [26] provided mechanism-level annotations, and peer-reviewed literature was retrieved from PubMed. All 1,307 evidence chunks were filtered to approved-release status and graded by evidence level (A, regulatory label; B, meta-analysis or systematic review; C, original study; D, in vitro, animal, or pharmacokinetic first principles; E, class-level mechanism inference). The approved distribution was A = 53, B = 191, C = 215, D = 584, E = 264.

### 2.2 System architecture and knowledge base

The production system (full_system) is a three-layer deterministic cascade with zero LLM calls (Figure 1).

**L1 rule engine.** Forty mechanism rules define DDI risk, including additive beta-blockade, beta-blocker–calcium-channel-blocker additive effects, additive alpha-2 CNS depression, CYP3A4 induction or inhibition, and QT-prolongation risk with low-absorption fluoroquinolones. Each rule is defined by entity-flag predicates with match-all or match-any semantics, and the highest-severity hit is retained (MAX-MATCH). The 43 policy entries comprise 40 rules plus 3 patient-factor policies; Table 2 lists the rule inventory with flags and match semantics. **No second-order interactions are modelled:** each rule is evaluated independently on the properties of the two agents, and no rule's activation depends on whether another rule fired. The only cross-layer composition is the final maximum of the rule and matrix layers (Section 4.2); combination products are handled by assigning the tier of the dominant component rather than by modelling component interactions.

Citation coverage is narrower than the phrase "citation-verified" would suggest, and we state it precisely. Every rule carries at least one source record, but the records are heterogeneous: 41 are DrugBank entries, 7 are regulatory label records, 2 are DOIs, 2 are DDInter pair annotations and 1 is a PubMed citation. Independent verification against three sources (Crossref, OpenAlex, PubMed) with retraction screening was performed for a subset: the verification report covers 8 manifest items spanning 7 distinct DOIs, and of these, three rules resolve to a verified literature citation (additive_beta_blockade, beta_blocker_ccb_additive, beta_blocker_digoxin_additive). The remaining rules cite DrugBank or label records, which were not independently re-verified in this work. The report is bound to a SHA-256 of the rule configuration so that any later edit invalidates it, and it is deposited with the manuscript (Supplementary Text S2).

Two consequences of that partial coverage should be noted. First, several rules whose output class would call for a citation declare a citation list that does not resolve to a verified item; the gate accepts this, so the phrase "citation-verified" should not be read as independent verification of every rule. Second, beta_blocker_digoxin_additive cites a study of additive topical-plus-systemic beta-blockade rather than a digoxin-specific source, and it grades as high a pair that its own DDInter annotation records as moderate; we retain the conservative high grade but flag the provenance gap here.

**L2 class matrix.** A drug-class matrix relates ophthalmic drug classes to systemic drug classes. The released configuration declares 21 ophthalmic row keys and 40 systemic column keys, of which 34 columns are used, and assigns a level to 104 cells, of which 83 carry an actionable grade (high/medium/low) and 21 are explicitly marked "none" and ignored by the engine; cells that are not defined fall back to a default cell. Four upgrade rules require both a class match and specific absorption or selectivity flags, for example a non-selective beta-blocker with high systemic absorption. An absorption-scaling step forces agents with low or very-low systemic absorption to low risk unless an upgrade condition applies; high or medium absorption preserves the cell level. This step is **not empirically validated** against outcome data: it is an author-specified design decision grounded in the pharmacokinetic principle that a dose which does not reach the systemic circulation cannot produce a systemic interaction, and its behavioural consequence is quantified in Section 3.4 (which reports how many registry pairs change grade because of it) and in the deposit's path-attribution audit. Where the tier assignment is uncertain, the pair is conservatively left to the rule layer rather than forced low. Note that this forcing applies within the matrix layer only; the final grade is the maximum of the matrix layer and the rule layer, as discussed in Section 4.2. Most matrix cells are therefore deliberately undefined: the matrix is sparse by design, encoding the 83 graded class pairs for which a mechanism or an absorption-based expectation exists (21 further cells are marked "none" to record that the pair was reviewed and needs no action), and any pair that reaches neither a rule nor a defined cell falls through to the L3 default-low path rather than receiving an interpolated grade. **Layer scope of the class mapping:** the drug-to-class mapping (113 ophthalmic agents, 232 systemic agents; `configs/drug_classes.yaml`) is consumed by the L2 matrix layer only. The L1 rules operate on mechanism flags rather than classes, and the L3 hard constraint operates on the absorption tier alone.

**Which layer produces the grade.** Because the cascade returns the maximum of the rule and matrix layers, the layer that determines each grade is worth stating. Across all 26,216 registry pairs the class matrix determines 68.7%, the G19 hard constraint 12.2%, the rule layer 12.0%, and an explicit default-low path 7.1% for pairs with no rule or matrix signal; these four paths are disjoint and their outputs are recorded in the audit deposited with this manuscript. The rule layer is not absorption-gated, which has one consequence we report rather than leave implicit: 202 pairs whose ophthalmic agent carries a low-or-below absorption tier receive medium (107) or high (95) grades, and every one of them arrives through the rule path. All 95 high grades come from a single rule, `nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism`, which requires no absorption flag. Section 4.2 quantifies that consequence and why we did not gate the rule.

**L3 hard constraint.** Low or very-low absorption with no rule hit and no matrix signal yields low risk (G19). For pairs with no rule or matrix signal, the default is low with a documented decision path. Unknown or ambiguous medications are not silently classified: the engine returns an explicit unknown-drug path and the screening service raises an error requesting resolution, so out-of-coverage pairs are never assigned a risk grade by default.

Drug-class mapping (113 ophthalmic agents, 232 systemic agents) was built from ATC codes, DrugBank records, and manual pharmacologic review, with DrugBank identifiers for 103 and 218 agents respectively; combination products were tiered by their dominant component. **Absorption tiers and their definition.** Ophthalmic systemic-absorption tiers were assigned from pharmacokinetic sources and regulatory labels, including nasolacrimal-drainage bioavailability for timolol [1], plasma-level measurements for timolol [27], and instillation-technique effects on systemic exposure [14]. The registry uses **six** tier values, not the four used in earlier descriptions of this work: `systemic_absorption_high`, `systemic_absorption_medium`, `systemic_absorption_low`, `systemic_absorption_very_low`, `minimal_systemic_absorption` and `systemic_absorption_none` (113 agents: 15 high, 17 medium, 61 low, 9 very-low, 7 minimal, 4 none). The L3 hard constraint fires on two of these values, `systemic_absorption_low` and `systemic_absorption_very_low`, as implemented in `kg_layer.py`; the remaining four values preserve the assigned level, and the final grades are unaffected because the other low-exposure values are already mapped to low risk within the matrix layer. The tier is a **categorical assignment, not a measured quantity**: the intended reading is fractional systemic bioavailability after topical instillation (high above roughly 50%, medium roughly 10-50%, low below roughly 10%, with very-low, minimal and none indicating progressively smaller exposure), but the sources report heterogeneous quantities (bioavailability fractions, plasma concentrations, or qualitative statements), so the tiers should be read as an explicitly stated ordinal model rather than as measured exposure for every agent. Two limitations follow and are stated here rather than discovered later: first, the current assignment contains at least one internal inconsistency that has now been corrected (flurbiprofen was tiered high in the registry while the curation file recorded low for the same evidence sources; it is tiered low as of this revision), and second, nine of the 113 agents in the ophthalmic registry are not topically administered (six intravitreal anti-VEGF agents, intraocular acetylcholine, and two surgical viscoelastics), so the topical-absorption rationale does not apply to them in the same way. Both are flagged as required corrections in Section 4.4; the per-agent table is Supplementary Table S5. **The table's source column does not evidence the tiers individually:** all 113 rows record the same string (`DrugBank DBxxxxx|PubMed`, with PubMed as a bare word and no identifier), and individual source records are named in this section for timolol [1,27] and for atropine and dexamethasone [29] only. The remaining tiers are the study team's curation assignment; Table S5 marks this explicitly so the support behind each row is not overstated. Ocular drug transfer from systemic administration is likewise complex and incompletely characterized [28]. For atropine and dexamethasone, tier assignment drew on regulatory labels and pharmacovigilance evidence [29]. The full per-agent tier table, with a source record for each agent, is provided as Supplementary Table S5.

The evidence base (1,307 chunks) was embedded with a 2048-dimensional Doubao embedding model (doubao-embedding-vision-251215) and indexed with LightRAG [21] (working directory data/processed/lightrag_index; 1,307 documents, 1,627 entities, 1,836 relations). Index freshness was verified against evidence fingerprints. Retrieval baselines used BM25 (Best Matching 25) [20] over the same evidence chunks.

### 2.3 Validation datasets and evaluation protocol

L1 literature-derived gold (118 cases). The set combines three subsets with different provenance, recorded per case in the deposited gold file (`data/gold/`, per-case provenance table `l1_gold_provenance.csv`). **Selection criteria.** The set was assembled to span the dose-route correction the system models rather than to sample representatively: it combines 20 cases graded in an earlier expert-informed exercise, 58 guideline-sourced cases chosen to cover classes where the systemic route and the ophthalmic route diverge most, and a procedurally frozen 40-case blind subset. **To be explicit about who constructed the gold — including the expert contribution.** Twenty of the 118 labels (G01–G20, marked `T1_literature_consensus` in the gold file) originate from an earlier, separately conducted exercise in which a clinical ophthalmologist on the research team of the study named in the Ethics declaration assigned grades with reference to the literature; five of these labels (G04, G05, G08, G16, G19) differ from the DDInter severity and follow that opinion. The exercise predates the 31 July 2026 frozen-set lock and has no separate approval or consent record; the deposited record does not name the clinician. The Ethics declaration discloses that one of the two 12-case reviewers is a member of that research team; whether the T1 exercise and the 12-case review involved the same individual cannot be established from the deposit, and we do not assert it. The remaining 98 labels were constructed by the two authors of the original submission (WG and YX) from FDA labels, Stockley's, BNF interaction tables and peer-reviewed sources; the source category is recorded per case, not a per-case citation. The two authors added during revision (HL and WS) contributed clinical input and interpretation of the results and reviewed the manuscript, but did not construct the gold and did not take part in the expert grade review. **Reference to system outputs.** The 58 guideline-sourced labels and the initial 40 blind-subset proposals were drafted without reference to system outputs. Three blind-subset labels are a documented exception: on 31 July 2026, after the subset was locked and before the expert questionnaire was drafted, B02 (high→medium), B12 (medium→high) and C17 (low→medium) were corrected to the engine's grades; the timeline, file hashes and consequences are given in Section 3.8, and all results are reported with the corrected and the pre-correction labels. **How the gold shares sources with the rules:** the sharing differs by subset — the 58 guideline-sourced cases were labelled from the same FDA/Stockley's/BNF statements that the rules cite, whereas the 40 frozen blind cases were labelled by the study team from the same literature without case-by-case reuse of a rule citation. **Contradictory evidence:** where sources disagreed we retained the more conservative (higher) grade and recorded the disagreement in the case file; the eleven cases where our label still differs from the guideline or from DDInter are listed with their rationale in Supplementary Table S1. We further disclose that one of the two 20-case blind halves (blind 2, cases C01–C20) entered the development loop through error attribution during class-matrix versions v2.1–v2.3; the other half (blind 1, cases B01–B20) was never used in engine development. Gold-level distribution: 28 high, 51 medium, 39 low (Table 4; all four datasets as Supplementary Table S8).

DDInter audit set (92 cases). A stratified random sample by absorption tier and DDInter severity. **Correction rule, as stated and as applied.** As stated, a case was to be corrected from the DDInter severity to low when the ophthalmic agent's registry absorption tier is low or lower, no mechanism rule fires for the pair, and no class-matrix upgrade applies; under those conditions the systemic-route severity cannot be reached at ophthalmic doses. As applied, 30 of 92 cases were corrected and 62 kept at the DDInter level, with three documented deviations from the stated conditions: (i) seven corrected cases (D3-048, D3-049, D3-057, D3-063, D3-078, D3-079, D3-081) are in fact reached by the engine through a mechanism rule and so fail the stated no-rule condition; (ii) the two aflibercept pairs, whose tier is `none`, were kept at DDInter although the stated rule would correct them; and (iii) the four flurbiprofen pairs were kept on the basis of the superseded high absorption tier recorded in the design file at locking — the tier was corrected to low afterwards (Section 4.4), and the audit-set lock of 31 July 2026 (SHA-256 8956cfe6…) predates that correction. Re-deriving the gold under the stated rule with the current registry tiers changes 10 of the 92 labels (`scripts/rederive_audit_gold.py`; result deposited as `outputs/recomputed/audit_gold_rederived.json`); under that re-derived gold the cascade scores 60 of 92 (0.652) with high-risk sensitivity 8 of 29, against 62 of 92 (0.674) and 8 of 25 under the gold as applied. Section 3.5 carries both readings. The audit is therefore semi-independent under either reading: the severity component is third-party, but the absorption correction shares the system's central assumption.

Development-phase and gold-calibration reference datasets (reported separately from the validation sets above). These three sets were used as development gates, not as validation: a development set (200 cases, DDInter-derived), an external PubMed-co-mention set (70 cases), and an internal validation set (78 cases). Their features are identical to the validation sets, namely the ophthalmic agent, the systemic agent, the agent-level flags and classes used by the rule and matrix layers, and a three-level risk label; the labels differ in provenance. The development and external sets carry unadjusted systemic-route gold, and the external set's co-mention gold was audited as noisy (literature co-mention is frequently unrelated to the pair). Results on both are reported in Section 3.2 with the explicit caveat that they quantify the ophthalmic adjustment rather than performance. The internal set served as the development gate; 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap it by case identity (gold independent, cases not entirely novel).

### 2.4 Baselines and evaluation protocol

Four methods were compared: pure_llm (a single LLM call with a domain-general system prompt and no retrieved evidence), naive_rag (BM25 retrieval plus LLM), lightrag (LLM with knowledge-graph context), and full_system (deterministic cascade). The three LLM methods shared an identical system prompt containing the three-level risk definitions and a general ophthalmic-absorption reminder; the exact prompts are provided as Supplementary Text S3. **Retrieval protocols, in full.** `naive_rag` performs BM25 retrieval with `rank_bm25.BM25Okapi` (k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75) over the same 1,307 evidence chunks that populate the index, lowercased and whitespace-tokenised on both sides; the query is the f-string `f"{a} {b} ophthalmic interaction"`, where `a` and `b` are the ophthalmic and systemic drug names — that is, the query includes the drug names. The top 3 passages are retained, each truncated to 400 characters, and they are prepended to the case as an `Evidence:` block. `lightrag` instead prepends a `Drug profiles:` block containing the knowledge-graph profiles of both agents, rendered by the curated `PreciseKG.full_kg_context()` function. That function reads the registries and rule configuration directly rather than querying the LightRAG vector index, so this is a knowledge-graph context baseline and not a vector-retrieval baseline; we describe the mechanism accurately and retain the name for continuity with earlier protocol versions. The exact prompts for all three LLM methods, the BM25 parameters, the parse rule and the temperature schedule are given in full in **Supplementary Text S3** and in the deposit file `configs/prompts.yaml`. LLM baselines used the DeepSeek API model identifier `deepseek-chat`, accessed through the OpenAI-compatible endpoint, with an output cap of 10 tokens and no chain-of-thought prompting. The endpoint does not expose the underlying parameter count or checkpoint revision, so we report the identifier as used and note that this is a single proprietary endpoint rather than a locally pinned architecture. **Input language was English throughout**, for both the system prompt and every case, in the form `Drug pair: ophthalmic X (eye drops) + systemic Y (oral/IV)`. Temperature was 0.0 for condition 0 and 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1.2 for conditions 1–4. The 10-token cap is a deliberate design choice that forces a one-word answer; it removes any possibility of reasoning or justification, and this bounds what the comparison can support (Section 4.2). We refer to the five conditions as temperature conditions rather than seeds, because only temperature varied; the deterministic method is identical across conditions by construction. Outputs were parsed by substring matching with an explicit unknown fallback; no non-conforming outputs occurred on the L1 or audit sets. BM25 used the rank_bm25 implementation (k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75, top-k = 3). Because the 1,307-chunk corpus also informed the gold labels, the retrieval baselines access gold-source material, which the deterministic system does not use; this asymmetry is disclosed and gives the baselines, if anything, an advantage. Five conditions × four methods × four datasets produced 9,600 prediction rows with zero API failures. Supplementary Text S5 records the run configuration and the file-level fingerprints needed to verify that the deposited predictions reproduce these figures. **Reasoning-model re-run (reported in Section 3.6 and Table 3).** The three LLM baselines were additionally re-run under an adequate output budget with a reasoning model. Setup, stated in full: provider Zhipu (Zhipu AI, China) through the coding-plan endpoint `https://open.bigmodel.cn/api/coding/paas/v4`, model identifier `glm-5.3-flash`, temperature 0.0, `max_tokens` 3000, five repeated samples per case, temperature held fixed so the repetition captures sampling variance rather than a temperature schedule. **Reasoning tokens count against the 3000-token budget**: the model emits `reasoning_content` before `content`, so the budget covers both. **Reasoning effort was not set**: the request carries only `max_tokens` and `temperature` (see `scripts/rerun_glm53_baselines.py`), so the endpoint's default reasoning effort applied to every call. **Zero responses were truncated** at this budget (completion_tokens never reached max_tokens across all 3,150 calls). No split vote arose: in every case at least three of the five samples returned the same label, so the majority rule never encountered a tie; the deposited parse rule is applied to each sample first (exact match, then substring `high` > `medium`/`moderate` > `low`, else `unknown`), and no sample returned `unknown`. The run was executed on 23–24 September 2026 and produced 3,150 raw responses, deposited in full at `data/predictions/glm53_baselines/` with the run script `scripts/rerun_glm53_baselines.py`. **Single-model caveat:** this comparison uses one commercial reasoning model at one endpoint; the endpoint does not expose checkpoint revision, so the finding that reasoning models adopt a conservative rarely-high-risk policy is a property of this model family at this budget, not a claim about reasoning models in general. The deterministic cascade requires no model call and is unchanged between the two settings.

Metrics. Exact three-level accuracy (Acc3; high/medium/low), high-risk sensitivity and specificity (high versus non-high), high-risk F1, and Cohen's kappa [30] with Landis–Koch interpretation [31]. Method differences were tested with the exact two-sided McNemar test on discordant case pairs (full_system versus each baseline; condition 0). Confidence intervals: Wilson intervals for proportions, Clopper–Pearson intervals for perfect subsets, and nonparametric bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples) for kappa. Standard deviations across temperature conditions are population SDs. No power calculation was performed; the study is a system-development report rather than a confirmatory trial, and all intervals are reported as descriptive uncertainty.

### 2.5 Expert grade review

Two ophthalmologists reviewed the assigned grades for 12 representative cases under a grade-review protocol (Supplementary Table S3; the instrument, with the experts' recorded judgments, is provided in the deposit). They are not an independent panel: both are clinicians of the Department of Ophthalmology of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, and one of the two is a member of the study team (see the Ethics declaration). We therefore use "expert review" and "perspective comparison" rather than "independent assessment" throughout. For each case the instrument displayed the drug pair, a short clinical scenario, the proposed gold grade, and a system grade together with a one-line mechanistic rationale; the expert then recorded their own judgment (high, medium, or low) on the same three-level scale. Because the proposed gold and a system grade were both visible, this exercise is a grade review and not a blinded assessment, and the agreement statistics below are reported accordingly. Cases were selected by the study team to span errata confirmations, upgrade disputes, downgrade confirmations, potency-granularity boundaries, and typical cases; the set was constructed around the system's outputs, which is why it cannot serve as an independent validation (Section 3.8). Mechanism-level evidence and references were not provided, and the experts were asked to judge from clinical practice rather than from per-case mechanism review.

**Collection of the ratings.** Ratings were collected on paper (12 cases across two pages) on 3 August 2026 and transcribed on 8 August 2026. The ratings were returned by the two participants and transcribed by the study team; expert 1's questionnaire is also deposited as a scan. Both participants' ratings are read from the deposited transcriptions, and the statistics below use those recorded values. The questionnaire form asked for the expert's independent judgment but did not describe research use or publication; the consent position is stated in the Ethics declaration. An earlier 12-item instrument prepared on 30 July 2026 (`phase4_*` files in the deposit) was never administered: its answer fields are empty in the deposited copy, and it is retained only as part of the process record. Agreement between the two experts, and between each expert and the gold or the displayed grade, is summarized with Cohen's kappa (unweighted, and quadratic-weighted on the ordinal scale low < medium < high). These coefficients are reported as descriptive summaries of a 12-case purposively selected panel; they are not a validation, because both grades were visible, the participants are not independent, and the sample is small.

### 2.6 Intended use and regulatory status

The system is intended as a research-grade, mechanism-based second reader for clinicians and pharmacists screening ophthalmic–systemic combinations. It is not intended to replace clinical or pharmacy judgment, and its outputs require clinical review. Out-of-coverage medications are rejected with an explicit error rather than scored. The shipped web interface carries a disclaimer to this effect. The system is not marketed as a medical device; regulatory classification for prospective clinical deployment would require formal assessment under applicable frameworks.

### 2.7 Software and reproducibility

The system is implemented in Python 3.11 (package ophthalmic_ddi_cds_agent). The complete source code, the curated knowledge base, the rule-citation verification tooling and the evaluation scripts are publicly available at https://github.com/wangguoqing932/ophddi-cds, together with a synthetic demonstration dataset and a one-command demonstration entry point. Engine, rules and gold versions are pinned by SHA-256 fingerprints. Because a file's digest depends on its line endings, the fingerprints are defined over **LF-normalised content**: a reader should convert CRLF to LF before hashing, otherwise a Windows checkout and a Linux checkout of the same file yield different digests (the deposited `configs/rules.yaml`, for example, hashes to 2afabaad81161c40 under the original CRLF line endings but to b5c77f6fde8a9f7d after LF normalisation). The evaluated version was rules 2afabaad81161c40 (CRLF as deposited); the repository-root `MANIFEST.sha256` lists LF-normalised SHA-256 values for the engine, rules, class matrix, registries, evidence base, gold files with their per-case provenance, and the LightRAG index build record (`data/evidence/index_manifest.json`), so a reader can compare any checkout against the evaluated version. All statistics reported here are reproducible from the released prediction files via the provided scripts. The report follows the TRIPOD-AI reporting guidance for prediction-model studies where applicable; a completed self-assessment checklist is provided as Supplementary Text S6. The system is a rule-based decision-support tool rather than a statistical prediction model, and several TRIPOD-AI items (for example model-updating procedures) are not applicable; this is stated per item in the checklist. Use of generative AI tools is documented here as the journal requires: DeepSeek-chat was used for the LLM-baseline evaluations and as an assistive tool in knowledge-engineering tasks (registry curation, evidence grading, and gold-label annotation), and Zhipu GLM-4.6V was used for visual verification of scanned expert questionnaires and figures. No AI tool is listed as an author, and all AI-assisted outputs were reviewed and validated by the named authors (see Declarations).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 System construction

The knowledge base comprises 113 ophthalmic topical agents and 232 systemic medications (Table 1; machine-readable form as Supplementary Table S4), 1,307 approved evidence chunks (A = 53, B = 191, C = 215, D = 584, E = 264; composition detailed in Supplementary Text S1), and a LightRAG index over 1,307 documents. The rule engine comprises 40 mechanism rules plus 3 patient-factor policies (43 policy entries), each carrying at least one source record, with zero policy errors (Table 2; full inventory as Supplementary Table S6); the coverage of those records, and its limits, are stated in Section 2.2. The class-matrix layer defines 104 cells across 21 ophthalmic row keys and 40 systemic column keys (34 columns in use) with absorption-scaled upgrades. Figure 4 illustrates representative system outputs for a curated set of clinical demo cases.

### 3.2 Development-phase and gold-calibration reference results

The deterministic engine achieved 100% rule consistency on the development dataset (Phase 1). On the development-phase internal validation set (78 cases, gold synced to the corrected blind labels), sensitivity was 1.000 and specificity 0.984; we report these as a development gate, not as independent evidence, because 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap this set by case identity.

Against the unadjusted systemic-route gold of the development set (200 DDInter-derived cases), the system achieved 0.210 exact accuracy (sensitivity 0.069, specificity 0.895); against the external PubMed co-mention gold (70 cases, audited as noisy), it achieved 0.443 (sensitivity 0.077, specificity 0.935). Under the evaluation protocol of Section 2.3, these low agreement values are expected and informative: they quantify the magnitude of the system's ophthalmic absorption correction relative to systemic-route labels, and they are not interpretable as performance. We report them to complete the record of all four evaluated datasets.

### 3.3 Evidence-graded agreement and comparison with LLM baselines (118 cases)

On this set the gold and the rules draw on the same literature (Section 3.9), so agreement measures the internal consistency of an explicitly evidence-graded encoding rather than accuracy against an independent standard. What the set does support is a like-for-like comparison of four methods on identical cases, and the ability to inspect every disagreement individually. Table 3 and Figure 2 summarize the comparison (five temperature conditions each; mean ± SD), and Supplementary Table S7 gives the same figures dataset by dataset and budget by budget. The state-of-the-art LLM baselines acting as comparators receive the same three-level risk definitions and the same general absorption reminder in their system prompt (Section 2.4).

The deterministic system (full_system) achieved 0.907 exact accuracy (107/118; Wilson 95% CI 0.841–0.947), 0.964 high-risk sensitivity (27 of 28; CI 0.823–0.994), 0.989 specificity, and Cohen's kappa 0.857 (bootstrap CI 0.772–0.934), with zero variance across conditions. Lightrag reached 0.737 accuracy, 0.779 sensitivity, and 0.936 specificity (kappa 0.603). Pure_llm reached 0.475 accuracy and 0.771 sensitivity (kappa 0.256). Naive_rag reached 0.415 accuracy and 0.757 sensitivity (kappa 0.182). Exact two-sided McNemar tests (condition 0) were significant for full_system versus each baseline: versus pure_llm (54 discordant pairs in favor of the system, 2 against; p = 4.4 × 10⁻¹⁴), versus naive_rag (60 versus 4; p = 7.4 × 10⁻¹⁴), and versus lightrag (20 versus 1; p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁵).

The accuracy gradient full_system > lightrag > pure_llm > naive_rag was stable across all five temperature conditions on this set. The accuracy gap between full_system and the best LLM baseline (lightrag) was 0.170, indicating that the deterministic knowledge engineering contributed more than retrieval augmentation alone. In this 10-token setting naive_rag scored below pure_llm, but that ordering does not survive an adequate output budget (Section 3.6), so we do not interpret it as a property of keyword retrieval.

In the per-subset analysis, the procedurally frozen blind subset (n = 40, SHA-locked) reached 0.975 accuracy (39 of 40; Clopper–Pearson CI 0.868–0.999); the single miss is flurbiprofen × warfarin, discussed in Section 3.5. The FDA/Stockley's/BNF mechanism subset (n = 20) reached 1.000. The guideline-sourced subset (n = 58) reached 0.828 (48/58). Eleven exact-scale disagreements remain in total: ten in the guideline subset and the flurbiprofen pair in the frozen subset. The guideline disagreements concern judgment boundaries, including QT risk with low-absorption fluoroquinolones, CYP3A4 inhibition with low-absorption macrolides or azoles, weak-corticosteroid potency granularity, and anticholinergic overlap with tricyclic antidepressants. Each disagreement carries a documented pharmacological rationale (Supplementary Table S1).

### 3.4 Registry-scale divergence between systemic-route severity and absorption-aware assessment

The largest result in this study does not depend on the gold labels, because both sides of the comparison are pre-specified: DDInter supplies a systemic-route severity rating, and the cascade supplies an absorption-aware grade. We therefore compared every ophthalmic–systemic pair in the 113 × 232 registries for which DDInter 2.0 provides a severity record — 1,933 pairs. Of these, 1,455 pairs (75.3%) were graded lower by the system, including 352 pairs downgraded from high (37 to medium, 315 to low; 18.2% of all pairs) and 1,103 from medium to low; 136 pairs (7.0%) were upgraded. Of all downgrades, 80.5% (1,171 of 1,455) were attributable to low systemic absorption. In other words, three of every four pairs that carry a severity rating would be presented to a prescriber differently under an absorption-first assessment, and the majority of that shift follows from one pharmacokinetic mechanism rather than from many independent judgements.

Two caveats bound this measurement. First, it quantifies divergence, not error: the analysis shows that the two perspectives rarely coincide for the topical route, not that the database ratings are wrong. Second, the absorption tiers that drive the downgrades were assigned by the study team from pharmacokinetic sources and regulatory labels (Section 2.2; Supplementary Table S5), so the measurement inherits that curation and should be read as a property of an explicitly stated absorption model rather than as an independent pharmacokinetic measurement.

### 3.5 Semi-independent audit: DDInter-derived set (92 cases)

On the 92-case audit set, full_system reached 0.674 exact accuracy (62/92; Wilson CI 0.573–0.761) and kappa 0.428 against the absorption-corrected gold (specificity 0.955). Only part of that set is independent of the system's own absorption assumption: restricting the analysis to the 62 cases kept at the third-party DDInter severity gives 34 of 62 (0.548), which is the figure free of the system's assumption and should be read as the audit-set headline rather than 0.674. Against the uncorrected DDInter severity labels, agreement was 0.370 (34/92). Stratified: among the 30 absorption-corrected cases (all corrected to low), the system agreed on 28 (93%); among the 62 kept cases, it agreed on 34 (55%) (Table 5). Baselines on the corrected gold: pure_llm 0.630, lightrag 0.591, naive_rag 0.535. McNemar tests on the audit set were significant for full_system versus lightrag (8 versus 1; p = 0.039), but not versus naive_rag (27 versus 17; p = 0.174) or pure_llm (18 versus 15; p = 0.728). We also report, symmetrically, that on this set the two ungrounded baselines exceed the system on high-risk sensitivity (pure_llm and naive_rag 0.848 each, lightrag 0.264, versus 0.320 for the cascade), so the cascade's advantage here is in specificity and exact accuracy, not in sensitivity. **That comparison is partly confounded, and we quantify the confound rather than leave it implicit.** Of the 1,307 evidence chunks, 500 are DDInter 2.0 severity statements, and the audit gold for the 62 third-party-graded cases is the DDInter value itself. Running the deposited BM25 retrieval with the deposited parameters, the top-3 passages contain the pair's own DDInter statement for 19 of those 62 cases (`scripts/naive_rag_leakage_audit.py`); for those cases naive_rag retrieves the gold label. Its exact accuracy on that subset therefore cannot be read as independent evidence, and the same applies in weaker form to lightrag, whose knowledge-graph profiles are built from the same curated corpus. The deterministic cascade does not query that corpus at inference time. We report the baseline comparison because it was pre-specified, and the leakage audit so that its weight can be judged correctly. **Comparison with the leaked cases excluded.** With the 26 retrievable cases removed, the adequate-budget naive_rag is correct on 43 of the remaining 66 (0.652) — identical to pure_llm on the same 66 cases — against 47 of 66 (0.712) for the cascade; on those cases its high-risk sensitivity is 1 of 15, against 3 of 15 for the cascade (on the 26 leaked cases it is 9 of 10). The retrieval baseline's apparent advantage on this set is therefore concentrated in the leaked cases and disappears once they are removed. **Gold reading.** Under the re-derived gold of Section 2.3 (stated rule, current registry tiers) the cascade scores 60 of 92 (0.652) with high-risk sensitivity 8 of 29; the conclusions above are unchanged under either reading.

High-risk sensitivity on the audit set was 0.320 (8 of 25 gold-high cases; CI 0.172–0.516), the lowest of the four methods (pure_llm and naive_rag 0.848, lightrag 0.264). This is the single most important limitation of the current system and is reported deliberately. The 30 disagreements with the audit gold were directionally consistent with the absorption-first design: 17 gold-high pairs were downgraded (8 to low, 9 to medium), 9 gold-medium pairs to low, 2 gold-medium pairs upgraded to high, and 1 gold-low pair each to medium and high (Supplementary Table S2). The four over-alerts (pairs graded high or medium by the system against a lower gold) were apraclonidine × midazolam, apraclonidine × loperamide, tacrolimus × ibuprofen, and bromfenac × enalapril.

What the system could miss. The 17 downgraded gold-high pairs include six pairs where systemic-route reasoning is weak on ophthalmic grounds (for example aflibercept × leflunomide, with no plausible topical systemic pathway). The remaining downgrades are clinically sensitive: betaxolol × verapamil and betaxolol × diltiazem (selective beta-blockade plus calcium-channel blockade), timolol × epinephrine, scopolamine or homatropine × topiramate (anticholinergic burden), and flurbiprofen × methotrexate. That last pair deserves comment because it illustrates how the absorption model interacts with the third-party labels. An earlier analysis of this registry tiered flurbiprofen high and therefore traced the divergence to a matrix cell graded below its gold. Reviewing the assignment showed it to be a data-entry error: flurbiprofen sodium ophthalmic is formulated at 0.03%, the lowest concentration of any ophthalmic NSAID in the registry, the other four ophthalmic NSAIDs are all tiered low, and the curation file recorded low for the same two evidence sources. Correcting the tier to low (Section 4.4) moves this pair and three related anticoagulant pairs further from the DDInter-derived gold rather than closer to it, because the systemic-route label is high while a 0.03% topical formulation cannot plausibly reach systemic exposure. We report the corrected direction rather than the original one: the divergence here is between a systemic-route label and a low-absorption ophthalmic agent, not between a matrix cell and its gold. For a screening tool, this false-negative profile is the clinically critical number, and it defines the calibration priorities in Section 4.5.

### 3.6 Reasoning-model baselines under an adequate output budget

Because the primary comparison used a single non-reasoning model with a 10-token output cap, we repeated the three LLM baselines under conditions that remove that constraint, and report both settings rather than replacing one with the other. The re-run used a reasoning model (glm-5.3-flash), a 3000-token budget, a fixed temperature of 0.0 with five repeated samples per case, and a pre-specified parsing rule (exact match first, then substring matching in the order high > medium > low, else unknown). The system prompt, retrieval settings and user-prompt templates were unchanged, so the two settings differ only in model, output budget and sampling. All 3,150 calls completed with no errors and no unparseable responses.

Accurate label agreement improved. On the 118-case set, pure_llm rose from 0.475 to 0.576 and naive_rag from 0.415 to 0.585; lightrag was unchanged at 0.737. **High-risk sensitivity, however, fell sharply**, and specificity rose to approximately 1.000:

| method | Acc3 (10-token) | Acc3 (3000-token) | sens (10-token) | sens (3000-token) | spec (3000-token) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| pure_llm | 0.475 | 0.576 | 0.771 | 0.250 | 1.000 |
| naive_rag | 0.415 | 0.585 | 0.757 | 0.214 | 0.989 |
| lightrag | 0.737 | 0.737 | 0.779 | 0.536 | 1.000 |
| full_system | 0.907 | 0.907 | 0.964 | 0.964 | 0.989 |

The mechanism is a shift in the predicted label distribution rather than an improvement in discrimination. Under the larger budget the baselines converge on a conservative policy: pure_llm assigns high risk to 6% of cases where the gold contains 24%, and on the audit set to 2% where the gold contains 27%. Because both gold sets are dominated by low and medium grades, this conservatism inflates exact accuracy while the baselines identify 6 to 15 of the 28 gold-high cases (the cascade identifies 27 of them). We therefore read the accuracy increase as an artefact of the decision rule the model adopts when given room to reason, not as evidence that reasoning improves ophthalmic DDI screening; the sensitivity column is the informative one.

Two further results from this re-run bear on the interpretation of the primary comparison. First, the ordering between retrieval methods reverses: under the 10-token cap naive_rag scored below pure_llm (0.415 versus 0.475), whereas with an adequate budget naive_rag scores at or above pure_llm on both sets (0.585 versus 0.576; 0.728 versus 0.609). The 10-token ordering is therefore a property of the constrained setting, not of keyword retrieval. Second, on the audit set the adequate-budget naive_rag baseline reaches 0.728 exact accuracy and 0.400 high-risk sensitivity, both above the cascade (0.674 and 0.320); we report this because it is the one comparison in which a baseline outperforms the system, and it is not explained by the conservatism described above. That advantage is confined to cases whose own DDInter statement the retriever can reach: with those 26 cases excluded, naive_rag falls to 43 of 66 (0.652), identical to pure_llm and below the cascade's 47 of 66 (0.712), and its high-risk sensitivity falls from 9 of 10 to 1 of 15 (Section 3.5). Its sensitivity is also unstable across the five samples (0.280 to 0.480), whereas the cascade returns the same grade every time.

Sample-to-sample variation was itself informative. At a fixed temperature of 0.0 the baselines still varied by 0.013 to 0.034 in exact accuracy across the five repeats, which is why the reported figures use a majority vote over five samples rather than a single call; the cascade's SD remained 0.000.

### 3.7 Determinism and temperature sensitivity

Full_system is deterministic: all five temperature conditions returned identical predictions (SD = 0.000 for every metric), a determinism check rather than a robustness claim. Figure 5 shows the per-layer latency trace for the worked example, in which the whole cascade completes in under 2 ms with no model call. The LLM baselines varied across conditions on the L1 set: lightrag accuracy between 0.712 and 0.771 (SD 0.020), pure_llm between 0.449 and 0.517 (SD 0.024), and naive_rag between 0.390 and 0.432 (SD 0.014), reflecting genuine temperature sensitivity in the LLM baselines. The same pattern held on the audit set, where the LLM baselines varied (SD 0.005–0.039) and the deterministic cascade did not. Because the five conditions differ only in temperature, these standard deviations quantify sampling instability rather than seed variance. The deterministic path exercised here is the one distributed in the release described in Section 2.7.

### 3.8 Expert grade review (two ophthalmologists, 12 cases)

Two ophthalmologists reviewed 12 cases under the protocol of Section 2.5, with the proposed gold and a displayed system grade both visible (Table 6; Supplementary Table S3; Figure 3). Inter-rater agreement was 91.7% (11 of 12; kappa 0.840, 95% bootstrap CI 0.526–1.000; quadratic-weighted 0.875). Against the grades the experts were shown, expert 1 agreed on 2 of 12 cases (kappa −0.176) and expert 2 on 1 of 12 (kappa −0.257); against the proposed gold, expert 1 agreed on 4 of 12 (kappa 0.020) and expert 2 on 3 of 12 (kappa −0.091). These coefficients are descriptive of this 12-case panel and are not a validation of the grading (Section 2.5). The two comparisons are therefore not "to the same degree": each expert agreed with the gold more often than with the displayed grade, because for two cases (R10, R11) the displayed grade did not match the gold (below). The disagreement was one-directional in both comparisons: against the gold, expert 1 rated 8 of 12 cases lower and expert 2 rated 8 of 12 lower (their discordant judgments are 8 of 8 and 8 of 9 respectively; R08 is expert 2's single higher rating); against the displayed grades the counts are 10 of 12 and 10 of 12 (10 of 10 and 10 of 11 discordant). Neither expert rated any of the four displayed-high cases as high (0 of 4). One case was fully concordant between both experts, the displayed grade and the gold (R07 levofloxacin × sotalol). The sole inter-expert disagreement was R08 (betaxolol × verapamil), graded medium by expert 1 and high by expert 2. The maximum achievable unweighted kappa given the marginal distributions is 0.294 for expert 1 and 0.314 for expert 2, so the low unweighted kappas partly reflect margin incompatibility. On the ordinal scale the quadratic-weighted kappas are positive — versus the displayed grades 0.355 (expert 1) and 0.313 (expert 2), versus the gold 0.473 and 0.438 — which places the divergence at roughly one tier rather than at random disagreement. With 12 purposively selected cases and a non-independent panel, these coefficients describe the tabulated cases; they do not establish a magnitude in any population, and we draw no inference beyond them.

**What was displayed versus what the engine graded.** For ten of the twelve cases the displayed system grade equals the deposited deterministic re-run. For R10 and R11 the questionnaire displayed **medium**, but this value was a class-level conservative hint written by the study team (the question text reads "系统判 medium（类级 IOP/全身相加）" — "the system grades medium (class-level)"), not the engine's output: the engine, both at review time and in the deposited re-run, grades both pairs **low** (rule path for R10, matrix path for R11), matching the gold. The design file's own system column records low for both. The deterministic cascade has not changed since the review: the only later edit to its configuration (23 September 2026) repaired a malformed advice string in one matrix cell and altered no level, and the core engine and rule files are bit-identical to the 3 August 2026 commit. Consequently the engine's grades coincide with the displayed gold on all 12 cases — but for three cases this is true by construction, and for two cases the form showed something other than the engine's grade, which is why Table 6 now reports both columns and why no claim of system–gold validation is made from this exercise.

**Three gold labels had just been corrected, and the corrections postdate the lock.** The questionnaire showed the experts the corrected gold for R01–R03, and all five L1 cases among the twelve (R01 = B02, R02 = B12, R03 = C17, R10 = B18, R11 = C20) belong to the 40-case frozen blind subset. ### 3.9 Limitations

First, the L1 gold shares literature sources with the rules (homogeneous construction), 20 of its 118 labels derive from an earlier expert-informed exercise by a study-team clinician (Section 2.3), and the remainder was author-annotated; only the 40 frozen blind cases are independent in a procedural sense, and only 20 of these (blind 1) were free of development exposure — while three frozen labels were corrected toward the engine's grades after the lock (Section 3.8). Second, 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap the development-phase validation set by case identity (gold independent, cases not entirely novel). Third, the audit gold applies the system's absorption assumption to 30 of 92 cases and deviates from its own stated rule in three documented ways (Section 2.3); against unadjusted DDInter severity, agreement drops to 0.370, and audit-set high-risk sensitivity is 0.320. Fourth, the 12-case clinician survey is a descriptive opinion exercise between non-independent participants, not a validation of grading accuracy: one expert is a study-team member, the proposed gold and a displayed grade were visible, the cases were selected around the system's outputs, and the sample is purposively chosen. The kappa values are reported as descriptive summaries of those 12 cases only. Fifth, headline results reflect the final engine on the final (errata-corrected) gold; earlier engine versions scored higher on the same cases (0.992), and the drift is disclosed rather than hidden. Sixth, evidence for ophthalmic-specific DDI outcomes is sparse, so a substantial part of the rule layer is mechanism-derived. These constraints bound the strength of the accuracy claims and are reported to support a fair assessment.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

The grade review in Section 3.8 is the source for the expert comparison discussed here: the same ophthalmic–systemic pair receives three different risk assignments depending on who is judging, and this study places all three on common ground. Systemic-route database severity and absorption-aware assessment disagree for 75.3% of the 1,933 registry-matched pairs that carry a DDInter rating, and that divergence is dominated by a single mechanism: low topical systemic absorption accounts for 80.5% of downgrades. Evidence-graded rules and expert judgment also disagree, and not because of the rules — two ophthalmologists agreed closely with each other (kappa 0.840) yet concurred with the proposed gold on only 4 of 12 and 3 of 12 cases, judging below the gold in nearly all discordant cases (8 of 12 for each expert), and their concurrence with the displayed grades was lower still (2 of 12 and 1 of 12); because the participants are not independent (one expert is a study-team member) and the case set was constructed around the system's outputs, this review is reported as a perspective comparison rather than an independent validation. What it establishes is that the assigned grades did not persuade the two clinicians. The results therefore support absorption-first knowledge engineering as a way of making ophthalmic DDI risk explicit and individually auditable, and they locate the largest source of disagreement not in any one tool but in the absence of a shared grading standard for this route.

The quantitative performance results bound that claim rather than carry it. The cascade agreed with its evidence-graded gold on 0.907 of 118 cases (kappa 0.857) and outperformed three LLM baselines (0.415–0.737 accuracy) with significant McNemar tests, zero variance across five temperature conditions, and millisecond inference. On the semi-independent audit set and within the primary 10-token protocol it led on exact accuracy (0.674 versus 0.535–0.630) and specificity (0.955), but its high-risk sensitivity was the lowest of the four methods (0.320, 8 of 25), and its advantage over pure_llm and naive_rag on that set was not statistically significant; under the adequate-budget re-run one baseline exceeded it on that set (Section 3.6), and with the retrievable-statement cases excluded the retrieval baseline's advantage disappears (Section 3.5). The procedurally frozen blind subset (n = 40) was classified with 39 of 40 correct under the corrected labels and 36 of 40 under the pre-correction labels (Section 3.8). Because the L1 gold shares its literature sources with the rules, the 0.907 figure is best read as the internal consistency of an explicitly evidence-graded encoding; the audit-set sensitivity is the number that bounds what the system can claim as a safety screen today.

### 4.2 What we add relative to prior work

Against general-purpose DDI databases. Existing resources such as DDInter provide comprehensive pair-level severity ratings derived from systemic administration [16,42] but do not encode the decisive ophthalmic modifier, the magnitude of systemic absorption from topical administration, nor dose-route corrections. Our system makes this ophthalmic pharmacokinetic factor an explicit, first-class modelling component [32]. A systemic-absorption tier is assigned to every ophthalmic agent (six values in the registry, two of which the L3 constraint forces to low); an absorption-scaling step forces low-absorption pairs to low risk within the matrix layer unless a mechanism-based upgrade applies; and a hard-constraint layer encodes the absorption-first pharmacology, with the final grade taken as the maximum of the rule and matrix layers. **That maximum is the honest limitation of the design.** The determinism that makes this behaviour auditable is reported in Section 3.7. The rule layer is not absorption-gated, so 202 pairs with a low-or-below tier receive medium or high grades through rules that require no absorption flag — all 95 high grades among them from a single rule, nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism. We could have gated that rule, as the four class-matrix upgrades are gated, and we measured what that would do: gating it lowers agreement with the L1 gold from 0.907 to 0.890, and gating all four NSAID rules lowers it to 0.847. We therefore report the behaviour rather than re-engineer it to fit the gold. That the gold prefers an ungated rule is itself informative: the guideline-sourced subset was labelled from sources that reason about systemic exposure, so part of the gold encodes the same systemic-route assumption the paper argues against. Whether to gate these rules is a calibration decision that should follow outcome-linked evidence, not gold agreement, and Section 4.5 lists it first. In the 1,933-pair DDInter audit (Section 3.4), 75.3% of matched pairs were graded lower by the system, including 352 pairs downgraded from high (18.2% of all pairs), with 80.5% of downgrades attributable to low systemic absorption. The methodological implication is the one worth stating plainly: a severity rating exported from a systemic-route database is not a valid ophthalmic ground truth, and any evaluation of an ophthalmic DDI tool that treats it as one measures the distance between two assumptions rather than the tool's accuracy. That is why this study reports agreement with unadjusted systemic-route labels (0.210 and 0.443 on the development and external sets, Section 3.2) as a quantification of the adjustment itself rather than as a performance figure.

Against LLM and retrieval-augmented approaches. LLM screening is non-deterministic and difficult to audit, and retrieval-augmented variants inherit the biases of their corpora [33,34]. Machine-learning predictors built on molecular structure [35] or knowledge-graph representations [36] instead target systemic-route interactions and likewise omit route-specific absorption. In our evaluation the graph-context baseline (named lightrag; Section 2.4) captured only part of the gain even though all LLM baselines received an identical system prompt containing the key absorption facts, and under an adequate output budget the baselines converged on a conservative policy that assigns high risk to 6% of cases where the gold contains 24% (Section 3.6). What separates the cascade from the baselines is therefore not label agreement but the sensitivity-specificity profile and the reproducibility of the decision. Our system is deterministic by construction — it makes no model call, so its reproducibility is a design property rather than a robustness result — and it is auditable (every decision returns matched rule IDs, decision path, and evidence citations) and fast (about 2 ms per pair on a single CPU), while retaining the full mechanism vocabulary of the knowledge base.

Systematic coverage of the overlooked space. Clinical recall of ophthalmic–systemic DDI concentrates on a small set of well-known agents, most prominently non-selective beta-blockers. Beyond this class, interactions are rarely mentioned in routine practice and are easily overlooked [37]. Our system screens all 113 ophthalmic agents against 232 systemic drugs exhaustively. It surfaces mechanism-based signals that clinicians may not spontaneously consider, including anticholinergic accumulation with tricyclic antidepressants, additive carbonic-anhydrase inhibition, CYP3A4-mediated exposure changes, and weak-corticosteroid potency boundaries. Each signal is traceable to verified evidence.

Validation discipline. Beyond absolute performance, we contribute a four-phase evaluation protocol with explicit disclosure of its own weaknesses. The protocol includes a procedurally frozen blind subset (SHA-locked), a third-party audit component (DDInter severity levels) with absorption-correction strata reported separately, a five-temperature protocol separating deterministic and stochastic methods, and full reporting of all four datasets including the unadjusted-gold reference sets. Homogeneous-construction, overlap, and development-exposure limitations are reported transparently. These elements are rarely reported together in clinical NLP or CDSS studies [38,39].

### 4.3 The expert–clinical gap: a prior-versus-evidence divergence

Two ophthalmologists — not an independent panel, since one is a study-team member — agreed closely with each other (kappa 0.840) but concurred with the proposed gold on only 4 of 12 and 3 of 12 cases, judging below the gold in nearly all discordant cases. The expert–gold and expert–displayed comparisons differ (Section 3.8) because for two cases the displayed grade was a class-level hint rather than the engine's output; neither comparison can validate the other, and the case set was constructed around the system's outputs. The exercise establishes a systematic difference between clinical prior and the assigned grades, nothing more. Three observations support this reading. First, clinically serious events from ophthalmic–systemic DDI are rare, and expert judgments rationally gravitate toward lower risk tiers; consistent with this, neither expert rated any of the four displayed-high cases as high. Second, the few interactions clinicians do recall are dominated by non-selective beta-blockers, which are prominently discussed in prescribing guidance; the systematic coverage described above is therefore most valuable precisely where clinical recall is weakest. Third, reports of serious adverse events attributable to ophthalmic–systemic interactions are rare in the PubMed literature, although pharmacovigilance analyses of ophthalmic agents do identify systemic signals [29,40]. High alert-override rates in computerized order entry [24] further suggest that a screening tool whose outputs sit one tier above clinical intuition will be treated as noise unless its mechanism-based justifications are presented transparently; our traceable decision paths are designed to do exactly that. Whether the gold, the system, or the clinical prior is the better predictor of patient-relevant outcomes cannot be resolved without outcome-linked data; the honest position is that all three are hypotheses awaiting prospective calibration.

### 4.4 Limitations

Absorption-tier assignment. The tiers are the most load-bearing variable in the cascade and the least independently documented. They are a study-team assignment (Section 2.2) with no operational measurement behind every agent, and one defect has been corrected during revision and one remains open. **Corrected:** flurbiprofen was tiered high in the registry while the curation file recorded low for the same evidence sources; at 0.03% it is the lowest-concentration ophthalmic NSAID in the registry and the other four are all tiered low, so the high tier was a data-entry error. It is now tiered low, and the registry-scale analysis in Section 3.4 reflects the corrected value. **Disclosed alternative tiers.** Thirteen further agents show the same registry-versus-curation discrepancy that led us to correct flurbiprofen (dorzolamide, brinzolamide, the timolol fixed combinations, dexamethasone, atropine among them). We adopt the registry (`data/seed/entities_a.csv`) as authoritative because it is the file the engine loads at inference time; the curation file is an intermediate artefact. To let readers judge how much the registry-scale headline depends on that choice, we re-ran the 1,933-pair audit under the curation-file tiers: the downgrade share moves from 75.27% to 75.69%, and the from-high downgrades stay at 352 (`scripts/audit_ddinter_disagreement.py --tier-override data/curation/entity_a_candidates.yaml`; result deposited as `audits/tier_override_scenario.json`). The registry-scale conclusion is therefore robust to which of the two files is treated as authoritative. **Open:** nine of the 113 agents in the "ophthalmic topical" registry are not topical (six intravitreal anti-VEGF agents, intraocular acetylcholine, and two surgical viscoelastics), including aflibercept, which is the example given in Section 3.5 of a pair with no plausible topical systemic pathway. Because the tier drives both the class-matrix forcing and the L3 constraint, the registry-scale divergence reported in Section 3.4 should be read as a property of this stated absorption model rather than as an independent pharmacokinetic measurement; Supplementary Table S5 lists the tier assigned to every agent, so the magnitude can be judged. Correcting a second class of assignments — the tiering of non-topical agents — remains future work, and the scenario analysis deposited alongside this manuscript quantifies how the registry-scale figures move under each candidate reassignment.

Bounded clinical impact. The scarcity of direct evidence plausibly reflects that ophthalmic absorption rarely alters systemic drug handling in a clinically detectable way. PubMed yielded few dedicated ophthalmic–systemic interaction studies or case series. The evidence base is populated largely by pharmacokinetic first principles, class-level pharmacology, and extrapolation from systemic data (1,307 curated chunks, but few ophthalmic-specific outcome studies). Ocular drug transfer from systemic administration is likewise complex and incompletely characterized [28]. If the true risk gradient clusters at the low end for most ophthalmic agents, an evidence-graded system may systematically over-grade relative to clinical practice. Conversely, the audit-set sensitivity of 0.320 shows the system can also under-grade relative to an absorption-corrected gold; both directions bound the current clinical utility. At a realistic 1% prevalence of clinically significant ophthalmic–systemic interactions, the audit operating point (sensitivity 0.320, specificity 0.955) would yield a positive predictive value of approximately 6.7%, so the system's value lies in systematic coverage and traceability rather than in screening yield.

Validation-related limitations. The L1 gold is heterogeneous by construction: 20 of 118 labels derive from an earlier expert-informed exercise by a study-team clinician, and the rest were annotated by the two designing authors from the same literature sources that informed the rules (homogeneous construction); it is a literature-derived proxy rather than an independently adjudicated gold. Only the 40 procedurally frozen blind cases are independent in the procedural sense, with 20 of them free of development exposure — and three frozen labels were corrected toward the engine's grades after the lock, so the frozen subset is reported both with and without those corrections (39 of 40 and 36 of 40; Section 3.8). In addition, 78 of 118 L1 cases overlap the development-phase internal validation set by case identity. The two-expert grade review is the only human channel in this study beyond the earlier 20-case exercise. It cannot serve as an independent test of the grading: the participants are not independent (one expert is a study-team member), the proposed gold and a displayed grade were visible to the reviewers, and the case set was constructed around the system's outputs. What the exercise does show is that two ophthalmologists, given no mechanism evidence, did not endorse the assigned grades and judged below them in nearly all discordant cases, with the divergence stable between them; with n = 12 purposive cases, this is a directional observation, not a significance claim. The ratings were transcribed by the study team from the returned questionnaires (Section 2.5). Headline results reflect the final engine and the final, errata-corrected gold; the earlier 0.992 snapshot is disclosed in the process audit. These constraints bound the strength of the accuracy claims.

### 4.5 Future directions

Four directions follow directly, with the first taking precedence. **Registry correction:** flurbiprofen has been corrected in this revision; the remaining work is to re-assign the tiers with a documented source and threshold for every agent and to reclassify or exclude the nine non-topical agents, then re-run the registry-scale analysis of Section 3.4. The tier table deposited with this work (Supplementary Table S5) and the scenario analysis in the deposit (audits/tier_alternative_scenarios.json) are the starting points. The same item covers the rule-layer question raised in Section 4.2: whether nsaid_antihypertensive_antagonism and the other NSAID rules should be gated on absorption, as the class-matrix upgrades are. We left them ungated because gating reduces agreement with the present gold, but that is a reason to revisit the gold and the calibration jointly rather than a reason to leave the design as it stands. Second, clinical calibration: a larger expert panel reviewing mechanism-level evidence, or outcome-linked retrospective data, could determine whether a systematic one-tier offset should be applied to specific absorption tiers or drug classes, converting the perspective gap into a calibrated prior. The audit-set false-negative pairs (notably flurbiprofen × methotrexate and betaxolol × calcium-channel blockers) are the first calibration targets. Third, false-negative surveillance: targeted pharmacovigilance mining (FAERS-style) and prospective monitoring of high-absorption agents would test the mechanism-derived rules against real-world outcomes and populate the sparse evidence base. Fourth, coverage and fail-safe behaviour: extension of the registry to additional ophthalmic and systemic agents, and a user-visible unassessed status for out-of-coverage pairs rather than a scored default, are planned. Fifth, scope extension: the class-matrix architecture transfers naturally to other specialty–systemic interaction domains (dermatologic, inhaled, and intranasal routes) with the same absorption-first design.

## 5. Conclusion

The system construction reported in Section 3.1 yields a cascade whose agreement with the literature-derived gold is quantified in Section 3.3. We developed OphDDI-CDS, an absorption-first deterministic cascade for ophthalmic–systemic DDI screening (40 mechanism rules; an absorption-scaled class matrix; a hard-constraint layer encoding topical systemic absorption), and used it to compare three ways of assigning risk to the same drug pairs. Against DDInter severity, absorption-aware assessment graded 75.3% of all 1,933 registry-matched pairs lower, with 80.5% of that shift attributable to low topical systemic absorption; a two-expert grade review likewise found systematic, one-directional disagreement with the assigned grades, in a comparison that — given the case construction and the personnel overlap — establishes a perspective gap rather than an independent error rate (Sections 2.5 and 3.8). The cascade itself agreed with its evidence-graded gold on 0.907 of 118 cases (kappa 0.857), with complete output traceability, outperformed three LLM baselines on exact accuracy in the primary protocol, and on a semi-independent audit set reached 0.674 exact accuracy — falling to 0.548 on the subset free of its own absorption assumption — but the lowest high-risk sensitivity of the four methods (0.320). That sensitivity, together with a literature-derived gold that is part expert-informed and part author-annotated, bounds what the system can claim today. The practical conclusion is that ophthalmic DDI risk currently lacks a shared grading standard: database severity, evidence-graded rules, and clinical prior disagree systematically, and evaluation and calibration in this domain should measure that disagreement rather than assume any one of the three is correct. Prospective, outcome-linked calibration is the next step, and the false-negative pairs identified here are its first targets.
